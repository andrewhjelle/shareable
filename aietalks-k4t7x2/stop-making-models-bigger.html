Stop Making Models Bigger, Make Them Behave

SpeakerKobie Crawford · Snorkel Watchyoutu.be/TNwJ1LMiENk
Post-trainingData curationEvals & benchmarks

If you only read one thing

  • Snorkel + UC Berkeley's RLLM team RL-trained a 4B parameter model to outperform a 235B parameter model (Qwen3-235B) on a financial-analysis tool-use benchmark, for under $500 and about a day of training.
  • The 235B model failed not from lack of reasoning but from poor tool discipline: it guessed at nonexistent database tables twice, got no results, then hallucinated an answer instead of inspecting the schema first.
  • Training on single-table questions only (not mixed or curriculum multi-table) produced the biggest uplift, and that same training regime still roughly doubled pass@1 on the harder multi-table benchmark, from 13.9% to 26.6%.
01The experiment: small RL-tuned model vs. large reasoning model

Snorkel's research team, in partnership with UC Berkeley's RLLM (Agentic Project) lab, set out to make a 4 billion parameter model outperform a 235 billion parameter model on a tool-use task for financial analysis, arguing that bigger models are sometimes 'a sledgehammer to crack a walnut' when the real gap is behavior, not knowledge.

“making a 4 billion parameter model outperform outperform a 235 billion parameter model on a tool use task for financial analysis”

“we didn't get the performance that we wanted with this right now. We'll just drop in a larger model”

▶ watch at 04:13
02Where the 235B model actually failed

Given a year-over-year revenue growth question, the 235B model queried a table that didn't exist, tried again with no result, and then fell back to hallucinating an answer rather than inspecting the environment's available tables and schema first -- despite having full reasoning capability.

“having not gotten anything back in either of those two uh attempts, it falls back to just hallucinating an answer”

▶ watch at 07:48
03Training setup and cost

The team built a self-contained FinQA RL environment (290 single-table samples plus a harder 79-sample multi-table 'FinQA reasoning' set) using GRPO on a 4B base model via the RLLM framework, published on PrimeIntellect, OpenEnv, and Hugging Face. The full training run took about a day and cost under $500.

“this is something that was able to be done like in a 24 21-hour job and the total cost of running that job was under $500 per run”

▶ watch at 10:55
04Results: tool discipline, not reasoning, was the fix

After training, the 4B model outperformed the 235B model, roughly doubling pass@1 performance; the fine-tuned model now checks available tables, inspects schema, and self-corrects on query errors -- the exact steps the larger model skipped. Single-table-only training produced the best uplift and generalized to the harder multi-table benchmark, where pass@1 rose from 13.9% to 26.6%.

“It's performs better than the 235 billion parameter model with that RL training loop. And the performance in terms of pass at one was essentially double of what it had been percentage-wise in terms of like solving problems”

▶ watch at 13:29

“it turned out the single-table only training was actually the one that yielded the greatest uplift for these kinds of questions”

▶ watch at 16:34

“the harder multi-question multi-table Q&A in the FinQA reasoning question set also saw 13.9 to 26.6 percentage jump after this training”

▶ watch at 17:36
05Using rubrics to target what to fix

Snorkel's broader eval approach breaks a model response's correctness into a rubric of individual questions rather than a single pass/fail signal, which helps identify the specific failure behavior (e.g. tool discipline) worth building training data for, even though RL itself (GRPO) still consumes a single scalar reward.

“building rubrics as part of our evals. And then those rubrics by breaking down the rightness or wrongness of a model's response into a full list of different questions that can be answered”

▶ watch at 18:37

Commentary (ours)

The single-table-only training regime generalizing better to multi-table questions than mixed or curriculum training is the most counterintuitive result here and worth replicating before trusting it as a general pattern -- it may be specific to how discrete the 'check tables first' behavior is versus more continuous reasoning skills (ours).
