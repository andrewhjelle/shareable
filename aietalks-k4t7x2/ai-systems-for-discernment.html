Build AI Systems for Discernment, Not Approval

SpeakerAngel Ortmann Lee · Duolingo Watchyoutu.be/CDqzWpwkSls
Product & UXSecurity

If you only read one thing

  • A Wharton study found humans exhibit 'cognitive surrender': AI-right answers boosted human accuracy 25 points, AI-wrong answers dropped it 15 points, and 80% of participants accepted wrong AI answers outright.
  • In a Duolingo English Test experiment, trained proctors accepted 50% of injected fake cheating-flag signals; rewording the proctoring guideline to require independent video evidence before upholding a flag raised the rejection rate 21 points.
  • Recommended fixes: split compound AI signals into separate questions (e.g., 'headphones detected' vs. 'violation'), design coding agents to behave like a junior developer proposing a reviewable plan, and calibrate interface friction to the stakes of the decision.
01Automation bias in human-AI review

A Wharton study on reasoning exams found that AI assistance either supplemented or supplanted human thinking without the human noticing: performance rose 25 percentage points when AI was right but fell 15 points when AI was wrong, and 80% of participants accepted AI answers even when they were incorrect. The talk calls this pattern cognitive surrender -- foregoing deliberation and adopting AI output with minimal scrutiny.

“for questions where the AI was right, the human performance increased by 25 percentage points. Whereas when the AI was wrong, it decreased by 15.”

02The Duolingo English Test experiment

Duolingo tested whether skilled proctors would catch a false cheating-flag signal or rubber-stamp it, by injecting a fake copy-typing alert into historical, no-impact sessions that had no actual cheating. Despite scoring above 90% on accuracy calibration metrics, proctors accepted 50% of the fake flags. Rewording the proctoring guideline -- stating the AI signal is only preliminary and requiring proctors to find independent video evidence before upholding a flag -- raised the rejection rate by 21 percentage points, without any change to the model or the underlying UI.

“they actually accepted 50% of these fake signals, meaning they were falsely accusing people of cheating half half the time.”

03Splitting compound AI signals

The talk gives a headphone-detection example where one yes/no prompt actually conflates two separate questions -- whether headphones were detected, and whether that constitutes a violation -- which produces bad training labels (e.g., penalizing a hearing-aid wearer). Splitting the two questions yields more accurate, higher-quality labeled data.

“if there's somebody who has a hearing aid, that means that the model correctly predicted that there are headphones or earbuds detected.”

▶ watch at 12:34
04Designing for discernment, not rubber-stamping

The talk recommends coding agents behave like a junior developer -- planning, asking questions, and proposing reviewable diffs -- rather than either dumping one giant diff or pinging for approval on every micro-change. General design principles include reframing the human as an investigator rather than a validator, and matching interface friction to the stakes of the decision so high-stakes review stays deliberately slow.

“Instead, you probably want a coding agent that acts like a junior developer.”
