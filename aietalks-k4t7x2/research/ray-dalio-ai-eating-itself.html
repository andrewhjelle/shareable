01The debt cycle and government finances

The host recaps that Dalio's earlier call for a 3% deficit-to-GDP target hasn't materialized -- the 2026 CBO estimate is about 6%. Dalio frames the answer through five intertwined historical forces (debt/money, domestic wealth gaps, great-power conflict, technology, and acts of nature), then walks through US government finances directly: the government is projected to spend about $7 trillion against $5 trillion in revenue, a 40% deficit, with debt at six times annual revenue.

“if we were able to cut our deficit to GDP to roughly 3%, we may have a shot at a smoother transition here. Today, the CBO estimates that the 2026 deficit to GDP is about 6%.”

02Gold's run and Dalio's portfolio math

The host notes gold climbed from $2,900 to $5,200 an ounce since their last conversation. Dalio reiterates his standing rule of thumb: absent any specific view on gold, a portfolio should hold 5 to 15% in it as a diversifier against the kind of debt-cycle and wealth-gap dynamics he's describing.

03Why Bitcoin hasn't played gold's role

Over the same period that gold rose 80%, Bitcoin fell 25%. Dalio argues this is because Bitcoin lacks privacy -- transactions can be monitored -- which means central banks, not just individual holders, are unwilling to buy and hold it the way they do gold.

04AI eating everything -- and maybe itself

Dalio says AI right now looks like it's eating everything and might eat itself, by which he means it may not generate adequate profits. He raises China's different approach -- treating AI usage like electricity, made free and open-source to drive productivity gains through usage rather than direct profit -- as a systemic risk to a US industry built on a profit-based model.

05Bubble mechanics: companies fail, technologies survive

Dalio draws a distinction he says people miss in tech bubbles: buying stock in AI companies is a bet on those companies, not on the technology itself. His pattern from history is that most companies in a given bubble won't survive, even as the underlying technology continues to advance.

“there's a giant difference between the behavior of the companies and the behavior of the technologies. And that the norm is in these is that a lot of companies won't survive in the start.”
