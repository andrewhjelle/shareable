The design process is dead. Here's what's replacing it. | Jenny Wen (head of design at Claude)

SpeakerLenny Rachitsky + Jenny Wen · Lenny's Podcast Watchyoutu.be/eh8bcBIAAFo
Productivity & learningAgentsCoding tools

If you only read one thing

  • Design mocking/prototyping time dropped from roughly 60-70% a few years ago to 30-40% today, with the rest now spent pairing directly with engineers and shipping implementation, as vision work also compressed from multi-year decks to 3-6 month prototypes.
  • Claude Cowork's widely-cited '10 days to build' framing undersells the work: Wen says it was 10 days to turn an existing internal prototype into something ready to ship externally, not 10 days from scratch.
  • Wen expects AI to keep getting better at design taste and judgment, but argues a human accountability layer persists (someone has to be liable for what ships), and her current hiring bar favors 'strong generalist,' 'deep specialist,' and 'craft new grad' archetypes.
01Design process fragments into execution-support and vision

Wen describes design work as stratifying into two types: supporting engineers who are shipping features rapidly with their own AI tooling, and separately creating direction or vision for where things should go. The vision work itself has changed shape -- instead of multi-year decks, it's now typically a 3-6 month prototype pointing people in a direction, because the pace of technology change makes longer horizons unreliable.

“there are basically two types of design work and design work is kind of like becoming really stratified in this new world”

“it usually becomes a vision that's like 3 to 6 months out and isn't necessarily something that is like creating this like beautiful deck that's like beautifully story told”

02Time split shifts from mocking to pairing and implementation

Wen estimates that a few years ago, 60-70% of her time went to mocking and prototyping. Today that's dropped to roughly 30-40%, with another 30-40% spent jamming and pairing directly with engineers, and the remainder spent implementing and polishing in code.

“the mocking up part of it is like 30 to 40% and then there's that other 30 to 40% there that is now jamming and pairing directly with engineers”

▶ watch at 18:25
03Claude Cowork's real build timeline

The viral claim that Claude Cowork was built in 10 days is, per Wen, misleading: the 10 days covered turning an already-existing internal prototype into something ready for external release, not the full history of exploration and design work that preceded it.

“Yeah, it was it was it was definitely longer than that. Like overall it was like 10 days to get it from what we had internally to something that we were ready to ship externally.”

▶ watch at 41:57
04Will AI get design taste? The human accountability layer

Wen predicts AI will continue improving at design taste and judgment, pushing back on the assumption that this will remain a durable human moat. She argues a decision-making and accountability layer still falls on humans regardless -- someone has to be answerable for what gets built, the same way an engineer remains accountable for code even when Claude writes most of it.

“I think it will get better at taste and judgment and design. Yeah. Like I I think we we might be holding on to that a little bit too much”

▶ watch at 28:10

“you know the same way that like even though cloud can write all this code for you today it is still an engineer who's accountable for like does that code actually work”

▶ watch at 30:43
05Hiring archetypes and internal culture

Wen's current hiring bar favors three archetypes: strong generalists, deep T-shaped specialists, and 'craft new grads' who are early-career but unusually eager and humble. She separately argues senior leaders should deliberately take on seemingly 'low-leverage' tasks like dogfooding and bug testing, because doing it themselves becomes high-leverage. She also describes using a 'legibility' framework (illegible founder/idea vs. legible) to spot early internal prototypes worth investing design time in -- citing an internal prototype that later became Claude's skills framework of markdown files.

“it's just somebody who is like early career and uh feels kind of like wise and experienced beyond their years, but is also just like very humble and and and very eager to learn”
