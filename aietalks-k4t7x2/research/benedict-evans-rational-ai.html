The most rational take on AI you'll hear this year

SpeakerBenedict Evans · Lenny's Podcast Watchyoutu.be/BD3vLtWhT5A

If you only read one thing

  • Evans argues AI labs' pricing power is not guaranteed -- models show no network effects, so the market could commoditize like AWS rather than lock in like Windows, pushing value to the application layer (c4).
  • He counters the anti-AI backlash with specific numbers: data centers are about 0.017% of US water consumption and roughly 5% of US energy, growing maybe a point a year (c5, c6).

01How big a deal AI actually is, and how early we are

Evans opens with what he calls his 'most controversial opinion': that AI is as big a deal as the internet or mobile -- a genuine platform shift, but not obviously bigger than those two. He extends the comparison to argue AI is currently at its own '1997' moment -- transformative in the abstract, but most of what people will eventually build with it hasn't been built yet, and nobody can reliably call which companies win the underlying race. The rest of the conversation is built around this frame of radical uncertainty rather than confident prediction.

“internet or mobile and only as big a deal as the internet or mobile.”

02Why you can't score which parts of a job are automatable

Evans rejects attempts to assign a percentage of a profession's tasks to AI automation (the O*NET-style approach), calling it fundamentally flawed and comparing it to the failed 1980s expert-systems method of trying to hand-build object recognition rule by rule. As a historical counter-example, he points to accountant headcount, which rose through the 20th century and again after 2000 despite continuous waves of automation, arguing that cheaper tasks often expand a field's total work rather than shrinking it. He also warns that exposure isn't predictable in advance: Uber, he says, displaced and then grew the taxi business in many cities, while Airbnb's measured impact on hotels has been comparatively marginal, even though both looked like the same kind of internet-disruption story going in.

03AI labs are hiring humans, not firing them -- and buying consultancies

Evans notes that the most advanced AI labs, including OpenAI and Anthropic, are increasing headcount rather than shrinking it, and are investing in professional-services and PE-style operations rather than simply replacing consultants. His explanation: implementing AI inside a large company -- redesigning workflows, connecting systems, retraining staff -- is itself a large project that companies don't have spare people sitting around to do, which is exactly the kind of work consultancies like Bain, BCG, and Accenture already get hired for.

“labs are the ones most investing in these folks.”

04Why model companies might not keep their pricing power

Evans argues that foundation models don't show network effects, so there's no obvious reason one lab pulls decisively ahead of the others, meaning competition -- and pricing pressure -- should persist rather than resolve into a winner. If the actual applications end up mostly built by other companies rather than the model labs themselves, he argues the market could look more like AWS -- commodity infrastructure with limited pricing power -- than like Windows, which used a dominant platform position to extract value from everything built on top of it.

05Checking the AI backlash against real numbers, and what to do about your career

On rising anti-AI sentiment, Evans separates complaints he considers real from ones he calls unfounded. He cites a Lawrence Livermore study putting US data-center water consumption at about 0.017% of total US water use, calling the 'data centers are draining the water supply' narrative largely false nationally, while granting it can be a genuine local planning problem in specific towns. He puts data centers at roughly 5% of US energy use, growing perhaps a percentage point a year, and separately warns that almost no reliable public data exists on real AI usage or job impact, since the labs themselves don't publish meaningful usage numbers. His closing advice to anyone worried about their job is to fully use and understand the tools rather than opt out on principle, since refusing to engage isn't a credible position when a law firm, for example, is hiring half as many associates as the year before.

Commentary (ours)

Our read: Evans' AWS-not-Windows framing for foundation models is the most falsifiable claim in the episode -- if it holds, the investable opportunity sits increasingly in application-layer and distribution moats rather than in the labs themselves, which cuts against a lot of current AI-infrastructure enthusiasm. His debunked-myths section (the 0.017% water figure especially) is useful, specific ammunition for pushing back on local data-center opposition. What we'd flag as least resolved: he never quantifies how much of today's enterprise AI spend is a temporary 'roaming data bill' anomaly versus a durable cost structure, which is really the crux of his own pricing-power argument.
