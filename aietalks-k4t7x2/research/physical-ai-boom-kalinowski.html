Why the next AI boom is physical AI

SpeakerLenny Rachitsky + Caitlin Kalinowski · Lenny's Podcast Watchyoutu.be/G5WTgB87rYQ
Hardware & devicesGeopoliticsAI industry

If you only read one thing

  • Kalinowski (ex-Apple, Meta, OpenAI hardware lead) says AI progress hitting a ceiling 'behind a keyboard' is what's pulling labs into physical robotics and manufacturing (c1).
  • Humanoid robots are still safety-limited prototypes; she defines 'at scale' as millions of units, which the industry isn't close to (c2, c3).

01Why physical AI is having a moment

Kalinowski says the AI labs and big tech companies are converging on hardware at the same time because they see digital-only progress eventually saturating, making the physical world the next frontier. She contrasts this with software's iteration speed: a hardware team effectively only gets to 'compile' a design four or five times in a product's entire life, versus daily or hourly software builds, which forces far more conservative, front-loaded engineering.

02Humanoid robots are still prototypes

Kalinowski says she shares widespread safety concerns about large, strong humanoid robots working near people, because the industry lacks enough real-world data to prove it's safe; she notes some designs, like 1X's Neo, reduce risk by pulling mass inward and staying soft. She defines 'at scale' as millions of units (or at minimum hundreds of thousands), and says today's humanoid robots are still advanced prototypes rather than products built for that volume, reliability, or low-intervention operation.

03The supply chain runs through Asia — and defense wants out

She traces the robotics supply chain from raw magnets through processing, actuators, and subcomponents, and says every layer of that chain has been outsourced to China, Japan, and Korea over the past 25 years, a shift she says she personally helped drive as an engineer. She argues the US specifically needs an independent supply chain on the military side, since drones and robot arms rely on the same base actuator technology, and frames this as part of a broader case for reindustrializing manufacturing capacity.

04Memory prices and the one-part failure problem

Asked about memory prices threatening consumer hardware and robotics, Kalinowski says she expects prices to roughly double, driven by AI demand and a supply chain that can't scale fast enough, though she won't guess a timeline. She also describes what she calls a 'catastrophic redesign': if a single constrained part like silicon or RAM in the needed form factor becomes unavailable, a company has to redesign its board and product internals from scratch and requalify the whole supply chain, unlike a swappable part such as a die-cast component.

05What changes fastest: war, not CAD

Kalinowski predicts more change will happen in warfare technology than in consumer electronics over the next two years, citing Ukraine's rapid drone iteration as the model for where military technology is headed. Separately, she says AI tools like Claude currently produce only surfaces or point clouds, not true parametric solid CAD with defined tolerances, so AI isn't yet doing real mechanical or electrical engineering design work.

Commentary (ours)

(ours) The memory-price and 'catastrophic redesign' points read as the most transferable warnings here — they generalize past robotics to any team shipping AI hardware in 2026, and her pre-buy advice is a concrete hedge.
