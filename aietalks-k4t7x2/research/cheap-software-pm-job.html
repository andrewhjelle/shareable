Cheap software made your PM job harder, not easier. Here's the new job.

SpeakerNate B Jones · Nate B Jones Watchyoutu.be/b6J387xJvHg

01The job shifts from building to classifying

The talk argues that AI doesn't remove the PM's job, it moves the bottleneck. When software production gets cheap, the scarce resource stops being the first prototype and becomes judgment about what should exist, get supported, or get deleted. That judgment increasingly requires technical fluency: the speaker argues there is little room left for a non-technical PM because AI products are technical systems whose behavior (model choice, agent loops, data access, latency, cost) directly shapes the product.

02Microsoft's internal AI sprawl as a preview

As a real-world example of scale, the talk cites Microsoft's internal Power Platform ecosystem: more than 1 million assets built inside the company, including thousands of agent/robot environments, Power Apps, and Power Automate flows. The framing is that this citizen-development sprawl, currently mostly internal, is a preview of what AI-assisted building will produce inside any company.

03The security bill nobody's counting

Faster, cheaper software creation has a downside: more credentials, local workflows, and integrations, each a place for access to leak. The talk cites GitGuardian's 2026 state-of-secret-sprawl report, which found AI service secrets exposed on public GitHub reached 1.2 million in 2025, up 81% year-over-year, and forecasts continued steep growth this year.

04A four-rung ladder instead of gatekeeping

Rather than blocking employee-built prototypes, the recommended posture is 'open discovery': ask what was built, who uses it, and what data it touches, then place it on a production class ladder (personal tool, team beta, supported internal product, customer-facing product). The talk warns that a ladder with no demotion path becomes a 'junk drawer' of unsupported obligations -- a new form of tech debt -- and recommends PMs stop asking only whether a team can build faster and instead ask what class of software they're looking at.

“the better posture for PMs to adopt is open discovery.”

Commentary (ours)

(ours) The four-rung ladder is a reasonable internal-tools taxonomy, but the talk doesn't say who actually staffs the classification work at the volume implied by 'everybody's job is to prototype' -- that's the open operational question a team adopting this would need to answer.
