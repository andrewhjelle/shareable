01How Anthropic built its governance armor

Ries, who advised Anthropic's founders early on, says the company wrote a two-tiered structure into its charter from inception: directors on its for-profit board are appointed by and accountable to an outside group of AI-safety-expert trustees who hold no equity in Anthropic. He argues this is why Anthropic can decline a Pentagon contract or hold back a model release without the pressure forcing founders out, and notes that public benefit corporation status alone doesn't guarantee good behavior but does block shareholder-primacy lawsuits.

“Anthropic has directors on its for-profit board who are appointed by and are accountable to an outside group of trustees who are AI safety experts who do not have equity in Anthropic”

03Harder is easier: Cloudflare's free SSL bet

Ries tells the story of Cloudflare giving away SSL encryption for free after an engineer pushed CEO Matthew Prince to live up to the company's 'make a better internet' mission, even though encryption was Cloudflare's most profitable paid feature at the time. He says the bet increased the top of the funnel by an order of magnitude and is part of why Cloudflare is a $70 billion company today.

“The top of funnel increased by an order of magnitude. In fact, to this day, people still talk about how Cloudflare is like the reason you take for granted we have an encrypted internet”

04What happens when governance fails

Ries contrasts two outcomes: Philip Morris's 1.1-billion-pound acquisition of UK inhaler maker Vectura, which was written down $900 million and dissolved within three years after the board felt fiduciary-duty-bound to accept the highest bid; and Nova Nordisk's foundation-controlled structure (dating to 1920s Denmark), where trustees once intervened to block a sale, an action Ries says ultimately created more than $500 billion in shareholder value. He cites academic research finding foundation-controlled companies like Novo and Zeiss are six times more likely to survive to year 50 than conventionally governed peers.

05The easiest fixes: file as a PBC, keep the team mission-aligned

Ries's most concrete recommendation is filing as a public benefit corporation, which he describes as a two-page Delaware legal filing with no real downside. He also argues mission-aligned teams move faster because they skip the ROI debates that slow down principled decisions, citing Clay Christensen's line that it's easier to do the right thing 100% of the time than 98% of the time.

Commentary (ours)

(ours) Ries frames his own advisory role in Anthropic's founding as a small, unclaimed contribution, but he's also the author of a book selling this governance framework and a co-founder of the law firm (Virgil) he recommends for implementing it -- worth weighing against how strongly he asserts causation between governance structure and Anthropic's growth, since the growth could also be attributed to model quality or timing.
