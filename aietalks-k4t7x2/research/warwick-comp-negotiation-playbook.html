01The baseline math of pushing back

Warwick says even a non-aggressive "what's the chance there could be a little more" produces close to a 20% improvement on an offer in most cases. Across his own client base, he targets about 40% movement on average and has seen increases of 100-400% from initial offers, most notably by breaking stated salary bands entirely.

“Almost always see a 20% improvement on that. And that is across the board from earlier stage positions.”

02Why not to negotiate over email

Warwick's central tactical rule is to never negotiate compensation over email or through an intermediary recruiter, because tone can't be controlled and a message can land badly if read at the wrong moment -- his example is a pushback email read by a CEO stuck in an airport security line. He recommends a video call or in-person conversation instead, where body language and timing can be read and adjusted.

“I can't control, especially when you're negotiating at high levels, if I push back, and the CEO that reads it's in the airport security line and pissed off and they read it”

▶ watch at 17:55
03Two case studies

Warwick recounts personally going from $14/hour to $120,000/year in a single negotiation early in his career, simply by responding to a recruiter's comp question with "what did you have in mind?" instead of naming a number first. In a separate severance case, a client's paperwork substituted "base salary" for the agreed "on-target earnings," cutting the payout roughly in half; asking "was that a mistake?" rather than trying to split the difference recovered the full amount.

“And so I went from $14 an hour to $120,000 a year in a single month a single jump.”

04Information and timing as the real leverage

Warwick frames the underlying asymmetry bluntly: a company like Meta negotiates thousands of times a day against an individual's four or five negotiations across an entire career, which he says should motivate preparation rather than discourage pushing back. He distills his approach to two variables -- information and timing -- as what actually creates negotiating power, and cites a study claiming that people who anchor a request egregiously high end up winning roughly 75% more than those who anchor at a "reasonable" number.

“Meta negotiates thousands of times a day. You negotiate four or five times in your career. That's it.”
