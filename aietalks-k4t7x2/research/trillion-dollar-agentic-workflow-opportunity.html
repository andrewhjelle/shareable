The Trillion Dollar Agentic Workflow Opportunity Is Here

01Why private equity needs a new pitch

Jones opens with a finance-industry saying that SaaS companies 'all taste like chicken' — interchangeable, easy-to-value balance sheets that made them ideal buyout targets. That thesis broke when SaaS growth and profitability metrics degraded because incumbents couldn't adapt to AI agents, leaving PE firms holding funds dated years out with no clear exit story. In response, both Anthropic and OpenAI have stood up their own enterprise deployment ventures backed by outside capital: Anthropic's with Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman, and Goldman Sachs, reported at $1.5B, and OpenAI's valued near $10B.

“SaaS companies all taste like chicken. In other words, all SaaS companies are the same from a balance sheet perspective.”

02The pitch: trillion-dollar value in finishing the workflow

Jones frames the opportunity as new: reliably completing an entire workflow at scale and getting to 100% completion, not just chat-assisting a step of it, is something he dates to spring 2026. He argues the value at stake is measured in trillions of dollars specifically because agents can now close out a full workflow rather than a fragment of one.

“The value we're talking about is trillions of dollars. It It's because agents can do an entire workflow.”

03Four kinds of pressure squeezing generic AI wrappers

Jones names four axes of pressure converging on companies selling generic AI agent products. First, frontier labs are moving down-stack: Anthropic and OpenAI used to just ship models and let others build around them, but now stand up their own deployment companies and products. Second, large consultancies (he names McKinsey and BCG specifically) are moving up-stack into deliberate agentic practices rather than just change management. He describes two further axes — systems-of-record locking down their own APIs, and private equity acting as a distribution channel — without quoting numbers for those.

“So, first axis, frontier labs are moving down stack. This has been widely reported and observed.”

04The implementation layer is the actual product

Jones defines 'implementation layer' concretely rather than as a buzzword: workflow design is deciding which decisions the model makes, which steps stay human, where handoffs occur, and what counts as done — a defined process with an owner, input, and output for every step, not a prompt. He pairs this with strategic advice for builders: sit closer to the 'business object' (the actual data objects and actions inside a workflow, like support tickets or sales-funnel stages) rather than betting on generic reasoning or summarization improvements.

“You must decide which decisions the model gets to make, what steps stay human, where the handoffs are, and what counts as done.”

“the key principle is to sit closer to the business object. Generic intelligence becomes valuable when it gets attached to the specific objects and actions that define real work.”

05Nobody owns this yet

Jones is explicit that ownership of the agentic workflow space is not settled: it is not a foregone conclusion that Claude, OpenAI, or anyone else will own these workflows, and he says real clarity is years away. As evidence the market is still being tested cheaply, he cites PE firms currently trying to see if an in-house team can rebuild a SaaS company's product over a weekend using Claude Code — an approach he argues can't actually replace the depth of implementation-layer work he's describing.

“And we are very much years away from having clarity there. It is not a foregone conclusion, for example, that Claude will own all those workflows. It's not a foregone conclusion OpenAI will own all those workflows.”
