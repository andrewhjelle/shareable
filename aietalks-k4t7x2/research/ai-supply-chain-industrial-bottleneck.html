Why the AI boom is about to hit a wall

SpeakerNate B Jones · Nate B Jones Watchyoutu.be/Poyi6X7rOwY
AI industryMarkets & economy

If you only read one thing

  • Microsoft is spending $190B in capex this year and still expects to be capacity constrained through year-end; Nate argues the real bottleneck isn't GPU design but the physical supply chain underneath it (HBM memory, packaging, power, cooling, construction).
  • Epic AI data shows the four largest AI chip designers consume 90% of global chip packaging and HBM memory capacity but only 12% of advanced logic die production, meaning packaging and memory, not chip design, are the binding constraint.
  • Nate's practical takeaway: AI vendor contracts are now supply contracts and need allocation terms, and buyers should ask three questions — reserved vs. best-efforts capacity, routing plans to cheaper models, and where human supervision is masking product failure in demos.
01Capacity constrained means something specific

Nate opens with Microsoft's Q3 earnings call, where Satya Nadella said the company would spend $190 billion on capex this year and still expects to be capacity constrained through year end. Nate's point is that this doesn't mean Microsoft ran out of GPUs — the constraint sits a layer below, in whether enough chips can actually be manufactured and packaged with the memory they need.

03The real bottleneck is memory and packaging, not chip design

Nate identifies high bandwidth memory as the single most constrained input in the AI supply chain — a company can have plenty of GPUs on paper and still not ship usable accelerators without enough HBM. He cites Epic AI's estimate that in 2025 the four largest AI chip designers consumed about 90% of global chip packaging capacity and HBM supply, but only 12% of advanced logic die production, meaning packaging and memory integration are the actual limiting factor, not the ability to design better chips.

04Power and construction are becoming board-level problems

The IEA projects global data center electricity consumption roughly doubling to about 945 terawatt hours by 2030, but Nate says the real constraint is firm power at a specific site on a specific schedule, not aggregate national supply. Traditional 12-to-18-month data center construction timelines no longer apply to 500-megawatt-plus AI campuses once transmission and interconnection delays are included.

05Efficiency gains are real but demand keeps outrunning them

Nate notes Microsoft said Copilot inference throughput went up 40% in the last quarter purely from software and hardware optimization, and serving costs are falling for equivalent performance. But he argues cheaper tokens create more token demand (Jevons paradox), and by his read the AI industry is currently in a bind where it has to keep building because demand is outrunning efficiency gains, not the other way around.

06The abstraction that broke, and what to ask vendors now

Nate argues the cloud era's winning abstraction — elastic compute that developers could treat as always available — is broken, because intelligence isn't infinitely elastic; it's bound by an industrial factory. He closes with a recommended question for AI investment reviews: what share of vendor spend is reserved capacity versus best-efforts allocation, and what's the concrete fallback plan if the default provider becomes supply constrained.
