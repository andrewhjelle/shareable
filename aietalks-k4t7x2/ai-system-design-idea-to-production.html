AI System Design: From Idea to Production

SpeakerApoorva Joshi · MongoDB Watchyoutu.be/T0HhO4YtTfE
Agentic engineeringEvals & benchmarksVertical AI

If you only read one thing

  • Define the business problem, constraints, and a SMART success metric solution-agnostically before choosing an architecture; jumping straight to an agent risks over-engineering.
  • Map data sources, update cadence, and retrieval technique per source, then pick the simplest design (RAG + control flow + human-in-the-loop for the claims example) and iterate from evaluation gaps.
  • Build evaluation and guardrail metrics in from the start, then separately optimize for cost, latency, and reliability once accuracy is acceptable.
01Why 'just wipe code it and ship it' breaks down at stakes

The talk opens by contrasting low-stakes vibe coding with building something real that other people depend on. Anthropic and OpenAI voices are cited making the same point: specs, not code, are now the hard part, because defining product requirements, system design, and eval criteria is what lets you trust an AI coding agent is building the right thing.

“the moment you're building something real, something other people depend on, something with real consequences, just ship it is actually kind of dangerous”

02Product requirements: quantify the problem, gather constraints, define success

Using a health-insurance claims-review system as the running example, the business problem statement should be user-specific, state the current state with baselines, and stay solution-agnostic. Business constraints (data residency, approved models, mandatory human review of denials) get gathered before design starts, and a success metric should be specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound.

“It should not prescribe what the system is going to be, whether it's going to be an agent, a multi-agent system, something else.”

▶ watch at 03:38

“Medical reviewers at MDB Health spend an average of 2 days processing claim review requests, which is four times the industry standard for non-urgent cases, and 12 times the industry standard for urgent ones.”

▶ watch at 04:08

“Any denial decisions must be uh reviewed by a human reviewer. So, there's cases which you can't fully automate with AI.”

▶ watch at 05:41
03System design: map data, pick retrieval, start simple

For each data source (clinical guidelines, coverage policies, claims history), the talk walks through update frequency, processing needs, and retrieval technique (vector/hybrid search vs. exact match). It warns against jumping straight to an agent architecture or letting a coding agent decide the architecture, recommending instead the simplest design that is evaluated and iterated from gaps found.

“it can be tempting to jump straight to building an agent. There's so much hype around them, or let a coding agent decide what the system architecture should look like”

▶ watch at 11:56

“start with the simplest design, evaluate it, find gaps during the evaluation, and iterate from there”

▶ watch at 12:28
04Evaluation, monitoring, and optimization

Guardrail compliance, response quality (faithfulness), a domain-specific metric, and system health metrics should be defined before shipping, then tracked in production for regressions. Once accuracy looks acceptable, cost/latency/reliability optimizations follow, such as semantic caching for similar past claims and structured outputs to guarantee citations.

“semantic caching could be useful to expedite decisions based on uh similar claims from the past”

▶ watch at 26:33

“Think deeply about your product's requirements before having AI generate any code. The product spec is the hard part now, it's not the code anymore.”

▶ watch at 27:04

Commentary (ours)

The framework is generic enough to reuse as a pre-build checklist for any AI feature, but the claims-review example glosses over how contested 'solution-agnostic' problem statements are in practice -- stakeholders often arrive already committed to 'build an agent' (ours).
