Ending AI Slop

SpeakerThais Castello Branco · Taste Labs Watchyoutu.be/lCBf9slCanI

If you only read one thing

  • Code is verifiable because of a property of code itself (it decomposes, verifies, executes), not because models are inherently better at coding; subjective domains lack this property, which is why capability lags there (c1, c2).
  • A fuzzy quality like 'great design' becomes gradable by decomposing it into codified components (e.g., a brand's colors, typography, motion) that an RL environment can check against, though LLM-as-judge alone isn't reliable due to reward hacking (c3, c4).
  • Because models predict the most likely (average) output and creativity lives at the ends of the distribution, optimizing for the mean causes the collapse that reads as slop; the fix on the data side is quality over quantity from selected experts, with commentary tied directly to the code component it describes (c5, c7, c6).
01Why subjective domains lag behind code and math

Castello Branco argues that code's verifiability is a property of code itself, not evidence that models are inherently better at coding, and that this is why subjective domains like design and writing remain harder to train. The thesis has two parts: capability follows measurability, so solving measurability unlocks capability, and models collapse toward the mean, which is a problem specifically in domains where the mean isn't the best answer.

“Code is something that decomposes, it verifies, it executes and so it makes it a lot easier for us to be able to train on these domains”

02Turning a fuzzy quality into a verifiable one: the brand example

Using Retool's brand as an example, the talk shows that 'create something great' is unanswerable, but 'make something on-brand' becomes tractable once the brand is decomposed into codified components like colors, typography, motion, animation, and textures. That decomposition becomes the ground truth for shaping an RL environment, though the talk cautions that LLM-as-judge alone is not always reliable due to reward hacking and hallucination patterns.

“let's say that we decompose this brand into the colors, the typography, the motion, the animation, the textures”

03Mode collapse: why the average answer isn't the good answer

Models predict the statistically likely next output and treat it as ideal, which is correct for math and code but wrong for creative domains, where greatness and creativity occur at the ends of the distribution rather than at the most likely outcome. Taste Labs works to counteract this by pulling training distributions away from the mean, including via a community of over a thousand experts across different styles.

“a lot of like what I describe it is a lot of greatness and creativity happens actually at the ends of the distribution. It's not the most likely outcome”

▶ watch at 07:40
04Data-quality practices for subjective domains

The recommended approach is quality over quantity: strict expert selection, forcing true distributions of taste rather than averaging disagreeing preferences, and tying an expert's commentary to the exact code component it describes, since models struggle to connect code to visual output on their own. The talk also distinguishes bad disagreement (on objective properties like alignment) from good disagreement (on subjective properties like aesthetics), treating the latter as a signal of genuine taste diversity rather than noisy data.

“if you can find for example a method to tie that exact code component to the commentary of the expert, suddenly you have data that is way less noisy and way more clear”

▶ watch at 13:52

“I would advocate for a quality over quantity approach. I think creating high quality data is expensive”

Commentary (ours)

The talk presents Taste Labs' own framework (decomposition, expert panels, code-linked commentary) as the solution without independent benchmarking of how well it actually reduces reward hacking or improves downstream model output versus other approaches (ours).
