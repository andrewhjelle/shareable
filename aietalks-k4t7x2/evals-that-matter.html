Build Evals That Actually Matter

SpeakerNick Ung & Akshay Sharma · Lyft Watchyoutu.be/3z2uT5aDx_Y
Evals & benchmarksAgentic engineering

If you only read one thing

  • Evals fail for three common reasons: scores that don't gate a launch/production decision, LLM judges that are too generic to be actionable, and no clear owner or mechanism to catch regressions.
  • Their first user-simulator LLM produced unrealistically polite test conversations and a 90%+ pass rate; fine-tuning it on real Lyft user verbatim made it harder and more representative, dropping the score.
  • LLM judges should be built and validated like binary classifiers: hand-label ~100 examples, split into train/dev/test, and report precision and recall against human ground truth rather than trusting an unvalidated score.
01Offline eval before production, online eval after

Lyft's pipeline runs a rigorous offline evaluation, including simulated multi-turn conversations graded by an LLM judge, before an agent is allowed to launch, gated by a defined criteria threshold. Once in production, an online pipeline traces every execution, runs online graders, and routes human-in-the-loop error analysis back to the development team.

“we have a launch gate, right? We want to make sure that we have certain criteria on our offline eval and we're meeting that criteria before we decide to launch this AI agent to productions”

02Three reasons evals fail

The speakers identify three recurring failure patterns: a grader score that isn't tied to a meaningful launch or production gate, an LLM-as-judge that is too generic and noisy for people to trust or act on, and the absence of a clear mechanism and owner to catch and respond to production regressions.

“the grader that we create, the scores that we create needs to be meaningfully gating something”

▶ watch at 05:47
03Naive LLM user simulators are too polite and inflate scores

Their first offline simulator used a frontier LLM to role-play the customer, which produced overly complete, patient, polite verbatim unlike real users, and yielded a suspiciously high 90%+ pass rate. Fine-tuning the user-simulator LLM on real Lyft user verbatim and defined user personas (bypassers, refund seekers, AI skeptics) made the simulated conversations harder and more realistic, and the evaluation score dropped as a result.

“our first attempt at our offline evaluation gave us 90 plus pass rate or accuracy rate, right? Uh this almost sounds too good to be true, and I think it indeed is the too good to be true”

▶ watch at 14:03

“we did see our evaluation score goes down after we have fine-tuned a LLM model that speaks more like a Lyft user, and therefore making our evaluation more difficult”

▶ watch at 15:36
04Generic pre-built LLM-judge metrics aren't actionable

Pre-built metrics like tool-usage appropriateness, response helpfulness, and conversation naturalness produced scores the team couldn't act on. They moved to task-level binary pass/fail rubrics built with domain experts and tied to business outcomes, such as an 'education rubric' that fails an agent for either over-explaining before escalating or escalating without attempting to educate the user first.

“the problem was these metrics were not actionable. They were not giving us any actionable insights. If something if let's say a response helpfulness is 0.5, then what do we do with it?”

▶ watch at 18:43
05Validate the judge like a classifier

To trust an LLM judge, they hand-label roughly 100 examples with pass/fail labels, split them into train/dev/test sets similar to ML model development, and compute precision and recall for the judge against human ground truth. They also flag that evaluation criteria drift as more data is seen, so judges and criteria need continuous co-development, and recommend adding confidence intervals when a score is used for a shipping decision or reported to leadership.

“eval should be framed around a task success or failure. And a binary outcome is very easy to calibrate and train um LLM judge that can consistently score your agent trajectory”

▶ watch at 19:46

“we can hand label around 100 examples with pass fail labels and then split the data into train, dev, and validation sets like how we used to do with machine learning models”

▶ watch at 21:22

“we actually discover what our evaluation criteria is by looking at the data and grading our outputs”

▶ watch at 23:28
06A continuous, not one-off, error-analysis loop

The recommended loop is to dive into raw traces, pinpoint failure modes, keep only metrics that actually change a decision, form a fresh hypothesis, and repeat on a regular (e.g. weekly) cadence rather than as a one-time audit. Tracing tools capture full graph execution, tool calls, token usage, and latency, and annotation queues let domain experts label examples without reading raw JSON traces.

“this loop is something which runs continuously. It's not uh not a one-off audit”
