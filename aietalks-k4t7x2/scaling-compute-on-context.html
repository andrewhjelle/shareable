Frames why models can't know your private/company data (they're trained only on public data) and walks through which technical approaches for teaching a model a fixed private dataset work versus collapse, including naive fine-tuning, KV ...

If you only read one thing

  • Models are trained only on public data, so they have no knowledge of what happened after their training cutoff and no knowledge of private context like your emails or a company's internal data (c1, c2).
  • Naively fine-tuning a model on a fixed private dataset drives training loss to about 0.0001 but the model then collapses and loses normal generalization ability when generating (c3, c4).
  • Since the data set is fixed and training from scratch isn't viable, the only usable scaling axis is compute-on-context itself; the most promising direction is self-improvement, where the model makes its own training data progressively harder as it improves, echoing AlphaGo (c5, c7, c6).

01The gap: models don't know what happened after training, or about you

The gap: models don't know what happened after training, or about you

  • Morris opens with Terence Tao's observation that AI has breadth across public mathematical literature but lacks the depth of years of specialized practice.
  • He argues this is because models are trained only on public data: they have no knowledge of events after their training cutoff, struggle with long-tail skills underrepresented in training data (e.g., AMD kernels), and have no knowledge of a user's private context, such as their emails or company partnerships, because that data was never public.

“one thing that models don't have is knowledge of what happens after they're trained”

said at 02:39

“ChatGPT and models like it are trained on public data.”

said at 03:40
02Naive fine-tuning on private data doesn't work
Slide at 11:00 · click any slide to enlarge

Naive fine-tuning on private data doesn't work

  • Morris walks through a concrete test: training a model directly on a fixed corpus (e.g., 10,000 financial reports) via next-token prediction until loss reaches about 0.0001.
  • The model then 'knows' the data perfectly in a narrow sense but collapses when generating, and cannot answer questions unless they're near-exactly encoded in the training data, so it lacks the generalization properties of normally trained models.

“you can end up with a model that knows the data perfectly well. And then when you generate from it, it basically collapses.”

said at 10:52

“it just frankly doesn't work. Like, just doing this kind of next token prediction on the data you have doesn't produce a model that has interesting generalization properties”

said at 11:24
03Only one scaling axis is available: compute, not data
Slide at 11:10 · click any slide to enlarge · 6 more from this section below

Only one scaling axis is available: compute, not data

  • Of the three usual scaling axes, more data, more compute/training time, and bigger models, Morris argues that when your data set (e.g., a company's private context) is fixed and training from scratch isn't practical, only the compute axis remains: how to apply more compute to the same fixed context.
  • This reframes the problem as 'scaling compute on context.'

“We can train them on more data. We can train them for longer or add compute. Or we can make the models themselves bigger”

said at 04:41

“This leaves us with essentially one axis of scaling, which is compute”

said at 07:48
04The promising direction: self-improvement
Slide at 15:50 · click any slide to enlarge

The promising direction: self-improvement

  • Morris surveys approaches (KV/context compaction, on-policy distillation, synthetic pre-training-style data, RL in unsupervised environments) and notes all eventually saturate once the model has learned the available data.
  • He points to self-improvement, exemplified by AlphaGo generating progressively harder training data for itself as it gets better, as the mechanism Engram is pursuing to keep improving a model's depth on a fixed private corpus rather than plateauing.

“AlphaGo makes its own training questions harder by getting better through training.”

said at 17:37
