If you only read one thing

  • Video generation is now cheap, but the hard problem shifted to judging whether it's good; frame-level tools (CLIP score, LPIPS) and general LLM-as-judge both miss whether the video actually tells its story (c1, c2, c3)
  • Character.ai distilled a slower committee of judges into a small VLM that scores a 15-second video in about 3 seconds, trained on relative pairwise comparisons (A vs. B) rather than absolute 1-10 ratings (c4, c5)
  • The first version confidently scored on vibe/gloss instead of the real axes (rating a static, non-moving shot 9.2 on camera work); pairing real vs. AI footage with matched encoding and annotation method fixed the data set without turning the judge into an AI detector (c6, c7)

The eval gap in video generation

  • The speaker frames the core problem as never having been how to generate video, but how to generate good-enough video and judge whether it's good enough.
  • Existing frame-level tools like CLIP score and LPIPS are good at checking whether a single frame matches its prompt or is consistent with neighboring frames, but general LLM-as-judge approaches are slow and highly sensitive to how they're prompted.

“the hard part was never how to make video. The hard part was how do we generate um good enough video and how do we judge if the video is good enough?”

02Frame-level tools miss the story
Slide at 02:30 · click any slide to enlarge · 6 more from this section below

Frame-level tools miss the story

  • Because video is fundamentally a storytelling medium, the talk argues evals need to check story-level qualities -- does the story hold, does the physics make sense, does a character walk downstairs rather than hover -- that per-frame consistency checks don't capture.

“does it tell the actual story? Does the physics make sense? Like for example, if you want a video of a character walking downstairs, does it actually walk or or hover?”

03A small VLM scores video near real time
Slide at 08:00 · click any slide to enlarge · 1 more from this section below

A small VLM scores video near real time

  • Character.ai distilled a slower committee-of-experts evaluation pipeline into a small vision-language model that not only scores a video but explains why (extra limb, physics violation, audio out of sync).
  • A larger model scored somewhat better but was significantly slower, and the team judged the added value didn't justify the added latency, since the goal was to bring evaluation as close to the generation loop as possible.

“it takes about the model we have trained, it takes about 3 seconds to score a three 15 second video.”

said at 08:24
04Train on relative comparisons, not absolute scores
Slide at 10:00 · click any slide to enlarge · 1 more from this section below

Train on relative comparisons, not absolute scores

  • Rather than asking a rater to score a video 1-10 (which produces high variance between raters), the team found people reliably agree on which of two videos tells a better story.
  • They trained the judge model on pairs, generating bad examples by corrupting good videos or making deliberate slop.

“we trained on pairs, right? Um uh A versus B as opposed to 1 through 10.”

said at 09:57
05V1 confidently scored the wrong thing
Slide at 10:20 · click any slide to enlarge · 8 more from this section below

V1 confidently scored the wrong thing

  • The first trained model was confidently wrong -- for example, scoring a shot 9.2 on camera work despite a completely static, non-moving camera -- because the training data taught it to detect general coherence and artifact-free gloss rather than the specific axes (storytelling, physics, faithfulness) the team actually cared about.
  • The fix was pairing real footage against AI footage with consistent encoding and identical annotation methods on both sides, specifically to avoid the model overfitting into an AI-vs-human detector instead of a quality detector.

“the frame you see here is from from a video that the model scored 9.2 on the camera work, and the camera didn't move.”

said at 10:29

“I made sure that the encoding is is consistent across both sides of of the equation.”

said at 12:03

Commentary (ours)

(ours) The "score compare, not absolute" finding generalizes past video: it's the same reason pairwise preference data underlies most LLM RLHF pipelines, and it's a useful default whenever you're building any kind of quality rater from scratch.
