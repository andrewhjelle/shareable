The Pipeline Is Dead

SpeakerIris ten Teije · Sky Valley Ambient Computing Watchyoutu.be/bRnoEpoK5m4 · 19 min SourceRecording of this talk at AI Engineer (2026), published on YouTube by the conference. Slides below are frames from that recording, reproduced for reference and study; rights remain with the speaker and the conference. takeAgentic engineeringProduct & UXCoding agents

Argues for architecting AI-adapted software as a canonical 'stem' plus bounded, individually reversible per-user divergences rather than one frozen shared codebase, relevant to anyone designing personalization or per-customer agent-drive...

If you only read one thing

  • Argues the one-version-for-everyone software pipeline exists because producing a correct change used to be expensive; as agentic coding drives that cost toward zero, software can instead run as a canonical 'stem' plus per-user, individually reversible divergences.
  • Responds to the objection that millions of AI-generated variants would be unmanageable by arguing brittleness comes from unstructured single artifacts, not from having many bounded, isolated, reversible divergences.
  • Names coordination (how updates propagate across per-user versions -- 'merge intent, merge outcome' not code) and desirability (proving an adaptation is actually an improvement) as the unsolved hard problems.

01Why software has always shipped as one frozen version
Slide at 00:00 · click any slide to enlarge · 7 more from this section below

Why software has always shipped as one frozen version

  • The speaker traces the entire software distribution stack (CI, packagers, registries, app stores) to a single assumption: software is produced in one place, frozen, and shipped identically to everyone.
  • She argues this wasn't a deliberate choice but a consequence of producing a correct change being expensive, so it made sense to do it rarely and centrally.

“That entire stack exists to solve exactly one problem. Get a frozen artifact, that code, from machine where it was built to the machine where it runs, safely”

said at 00:00

“encodes the same assumption. Software is produced in one place. It runs in another. Anything in the middle, the artifact, is frozen”

02What's changing: the cost of a correct change is collapsing
Slide at 03:40 · click any slide to enlarge · 8 more from this section below

What's changing: the cost of a correct change is collapsing

  • Beyond AI writing code, the talk frames the shift as the cost of producing a correct, scoped change collapsing toward zero, and production no longer needing to happen in one place before anyone runs the software -- it can happen server-side, client-side, or live in a user's session.

“The cost of producing a correct and scope change is collapsing towards zero and just as importantly the production of the software no longer has to happen in one place up front before anyone runs it”

said at 03:39
03The proposed architecture: a stem plus bounded divergences
Slide at 09:00 · click any slide to enlarge

The proposed architecture: a stem plus bounded divergences

  • Instead of one codebase gated by feature flags, the company's model is one canonical 'stem' with per-user divergences that are bounded, isolated, and individually reversible, so a bad variant can't corrupt the stem or spread to other users.
  • Developers can still set hard boundaries, e.g. marking auth or payments fields as always off-limits to adaptation.

“You've got a stem plus divergences. The divergences are bounded, isolated, and individually reversible”

said at 09:25

“indicate that specific fields can never be dropped or parts of your app, like off or payments, should always be out of um uh uh should always be off limits for any sort of adaptation”

04Addressing the obvious objection
Slide at 10:30 · click any slide to enlarge · 8 more from this section below

Addressing the obvious objection

  • A CTO objected that if he can barely reason about one AI-generated codebase, running millions of them multiplies his worst problem.
  • The speaker's response is that the described brittleness comes from unmanaged divergence inside a single unstructured artifact, not from AI generation itself, and that bounded per-user divergences avoid that failure mode.

“I can already barely reason about one AI-generated code base. And you want me to run millions of these? You're not describing a capability. You're describing my worst problem multiplied”

said at 08:54
05Open problems: coordination and desirability
Slide at 16:50 · click any slide to enlarge · 6 more from this section below

Open problems: coordination and desirability

  • The hardest unsolved parts are named as coordination (propagating updates across a million per-user versions, framed as 'don't merge code, merge intent, merge outcome') and desirability (proving a given code change is actually a good change, not just a correct one, against metrics like retention or support tickets).

“the answer that we keep coming back to is don't merge code, merge intent, merge outcome”

said at 17:08

“We spent 20 years getting good at shipping one version for everyone. The next 20 years are about shipping the right version to anyone with the isolation and provenance that makes it safe instead of terrifying”

said at 19:15

Commentary (ours)

The 'stem plus bounded divergence' framing is essentially git branching plus feature flags reinvented for a world where the branch-per-user is generated live by an agent rather than hand-written; the interesting open question the talk doesn't fully answer is who audits desirability at scale once autonomy increases past recommendation-only (ours).
