If you only read one thing

  • Published AI benchmarks are often not reproducible because labs don't disclose model configuration or how they optimized for the test (c3), and only ~30,000 AI researchers create evals for ~30 million engineers, leaving huge swaths of use cases unbenchmarked (c4).
  • Kaggle's Game Arena avoids benchmark saturation by running PvP model-vs-model games (Werewolf, poker, chess), but getting statistical significance required about 400,000 poker hands (c6, c7).
  • Harness choice matters more than people assume: six frontier models scored within a couple percentage points of each other on SWE-bench Pro, but performance varied by 22% depending on which harness ran them (c9).

01Why published benchmarks mislead

Why published benchmarks mislead

  • Nick Kang argues evals are scattered and go stale fast because 10+ new benchmarks drop daily and authors move on after publishing.
  • He gives a specific anecdote: Kaggle published a benchmark with one AI lab, a competing lab re-ran it and published much higher results because they used API-provided compaction that Kaggle hadn't applied to any model, meaning the published numbers didn't reflect the actual state of things.

“basically like 10 plus of them drop every single day, and the best way to find out what they are is go to archive and spend like hours scrolling through them”

“they ran it, and then they published it with much higher, much better results. And the difference was that they were optimizing it for their model”

02Only a small group of people build evals for everyone
Slide at 02:40 · click any slide to enlarge · 8 more from this section below

Only a small group of people build evals for everyone

  • Kang contrasts roughly 30,000 AI researchers against roughly 30 million software engineers and technical professionals, arguing this imbalance means most use cases go unevaluated and drives uneven ('jagged') model capability.
  • He cites a Turkish wastewater-treatment-plant engineer who built his own proprietary benchmark after safety incidents at his facility, because that use case doesn't exist anywhere else on the web.

“They're like something like 30,000 AI researchers, at least that's what Google AI search told me, and I think there's like 30 million software engineers, data scientists, technical folks”

said at 03:36

“he built this benchmark cuz he cares and he recounted a story where, you know, he's been doing this for 20 years. There have been severe incidents in his country”

03Game Arena avoids saturation but costs a lot to run
Slide at 12:30 · click any slide to enlarge

Game Arena avoids saturation but costs a lot to run

  • Kaggle's Game Arena benchmarks models against each other in PvP games (Werewolf for deception, poker for risk tolerance and randomization, chess) so no single model can permanently saturate the leaderboard.
  • Michael Aaron says newer-generation models tend to play poker more conservatively/risk-averse than older ones, and reaching statistical significance for poker required running about 400,000 hands, driving them to use Bradley-Terry pairwise comparison to cut down on required game volume.

“some of the newer generation of models are worse at poker because they are more risk-averse. And so, you just see these personalities uh start to emerge over time”

said at 12:52

“for poker in order to get statistical significance, we had to run about 400,000 poker hands. Um and there's many turns inside of each of those hands”

said at 14:59

“it uses Bradley-Terry pairwise to try to like not have too many games we have to run. I'll talk about that in a second”

said at 14:28
04Standardized agent exams launched a week ago, already 500+ agents tested
Slide at 11:40 · click any slide to enlarge · 8 more from this section below

Standardized agent exams launched a week ago, already 500+ agents tested

  • Kaggle launched an MVP letting anyone paste a one-line prompt into their agent and get a leaderboard score; within a week over 500 agents had been evaluated with minimal promotion, which Aaron and Kang read as evidence of consumer demand for agent evaluation, especially given most people don't test their own consumer agents before deployment.

“we only launched a week ago, and we have, you know, hundreds, like 500 plus agents already evaluated on our exam, um, without us even really promoting it very much”

said at 11:16
05Agentic benchmarking conflates model and harness
Slide at 19:20 · click any slide to enlarge · 2 more from this section below

Agentic benchmarking conflates model and harness

  • As Kaggle's benchmark platform shifted from evaluating models to evaluating agents, Aaron cites a Morph LLM blog post finding six frontier models scored within a couple percentage points of each other on SWE-bench Pro, while the harness running them caused up to a 22% performance swing, raising the question of whether a benchmark is really testing the model or the harness.

“against SWE-bench Pro the six frontier models are within a couple of percentage points of each other”

said at 18:37

Commentary (ours)

The harness-vs-model conflation point (22% swing from harness choice alone) undercuts a lot of leaderboard-driven model selection happening right now; teams comparing frontier models on coding benchmarks may be more accurately comparing harnesses (ours).
