Agents Don't Do Standups: Building the Post-Engineer Engineering Org

SpeakerMike Spitz · PFF Watchyoutu.be/VMemhtlsoNk · 17 min SourceRecording of this talk at AI Engineer (2026), published on YouTube by the conference. Quotes are verbatim from the talk transcript with the time they were said.
Blueprint · full operating modelAgentic engineeringCoding agents

PFF restructured its engineering org around agents: a two-person pod deploying 5x per day with a 10x complexity-weighted output metric, dropping Scrum, and reassigning code-review nits to agents.

If you only read one thing

  • PFF's two-person AI-augmented pod deployed roughly 5x/day versus about once every five days for a roughly 10-engineer team, and a blended tickets-plus-complexity metric put the pod's output at 10x.
  • Customer-survey quality scores rose to 8.6 out of 10 after the shift, versus about 7 to 7.5 before AI.
  • PFF dropped Scrum ceremonies entirely (no project manager, no sprint planning) in favor of every-other-day huddles, and now assigns agents specifically to the code-review nitpicks engineers hated giving each other, like variable naming and style.

This traces PFF's own case-study numbers into a structure: a two-person AI-augmented pod replaced Scrum ceremonies with agent-run review and short huddles, and the three metrics it reports (blended output, build time, customer quality) all trace back to that pod. It's a single self-reported comparison, not an external benchmark (ours).

Why this talk is a blueprint, not a take (ours)

Scope: PFF's restructuring of a small engineering pod's process around coding agents, from spec through ticketing, code review, QA, and the metrics used to judge it.

A phased pattern for offloading process (auto-generated LDDs, tickets, and status updates; agent-run style nits in code review; an agent-run QA pass against acceptance criteria) while keeping humans on design decisions and every-other-day huddles for feedback. PFF pairs this with a customer-survey quality metric rather than relying on deploy counts alone, reporting 8.6 out of 10 versus 7 to 7.5 before AI.

Prerequisites the talk implies:

  • Coding agents integrated deeply enough to generate specs, lightweight design documents, tickets, and PRs, not just write code
  • A skill or prior-LDD corpus the spec agent can draw on so new designs match the org's existing ethos
  • A staging environment and defined acceptance criteria a QA agent can check tickets against
  • Engineers picked for curiosity and comfort with ambiguity rather than a need for highly prescriptive specs
  • A customer feedback or survey mechanism to substitute for the retrospective and validate output quality

What the talk does not say:

  • Team size or roles beyond the two-engineer pod and the comparison team, such as who runs the huddles
  • How the blended tickets-plus-complexity metric is actually calculated
  • Cost of the coding-agent tokens, beyond the passing mention that a current token subsidy will eventually end
  • Failure rate or error cases where the QA agent or code-review agent got something wrong
  • How results would generalize past a 20-engineer company to a larger organization, which the speaker explicitly flags as harder

If you were to replicate one thing first: Start by picking the most curious engineers and a non-critical system to run a slow, phased experiment in, rather than rolling coding agents out to the whole team at once.
