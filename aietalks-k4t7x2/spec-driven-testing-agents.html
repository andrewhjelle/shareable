Spec-Driven Testing for Agents With A Brain the Size of A Planet

Argues that a dataset-based eval (inputs/outputs, F1/accuracy) is insufficient to specify what an agent should do, and lays out the additional spec components -- business rules, ontologies, domain terminology, roles/permissions, and robu...

If you only read one thing

  • Bigger/smarter models aren't automatically safer: some jailbreaks (e.g. instructions encoded in a poem) work on large models specifically because they're smart enough to decode and execute them, while a smaller model wouldn't understand it, and broader-remit agents create larger attack surface (c1, c2).
  • A full agent spec needs business rules (e.g. never give a discount over 10%, no refunds after 30 days), domain terminology (gross profit vs. gross sales), and robustness ranges (how many typos or how much rephrasing before behavior degrades), not just a labeled dataset (c4, c5, c6).
  • Knowing the spec also tells you where an agent is most likely vulnerable, since it will engage on the domains and hold the permissions it's built for (c7); tests should stay implementation-independent so they survive a switch of underlying agent infrastructure (c8).

Bigger and smarter isn't automatically safer

  • The speaker (using the Hitchhiker's Guide robot Marvin as a framing device) argues a smarter/bigger model isn't automatically a better or safer agent: some jailbreaks succeed specifically because a larger model is smart enough to decode an instruction hidden in, e.g., a poem, whereas a smaller model wouldn't understand it well enough to execute it.

02The smart/safe tradeoff, and cost
Slide at 02:00 · click any slide to enlarge

The smart/safe tradeoff, and cost

  • There's a tradeoff between an agent being smart/capable and being safe, further complicated by cost -- using a large model for something simple like basic math wastes tokens and adds latency.
  • What's needed is a model good enough to perform the task but not capable of arbitrary harm, where 'harm' spans both what instructions it will accept and what tools/actions it can actually execute in a company's infrastructure.

“there's this trade-off between smart and safe in some sense and um smart and and capable in the other direction.”

said at 03:40
03A dataset alone doesn't specify agent behavior
Slide at 02:00 · click any slide to enlarge

A dataset alone doesn't specify agent behavior

  • Beyond a ground-truth dataset, a real spec needs explicit business rules (e.g. never give more than a 10% discount, no refunds after 30 days past purchase), ontologies/domain vocabulary the agent must know (e.g. an airline's actual destination list), internal company terminology, and domain-specific term distinctions that a general LLM would conflate (e.g. gross profit vs. gross sales), plus role- and permission-based behavior differences.

“don't ever give a discount more than 10%. Uh we don't allow refunds if um if you know, it's more than 30 days past the purchase”

said at 05:15

“if you're sort of talking to an LLM generally, it might actually confuse those two terms. But in business they're very different things.”

said at 06:20
04Robustness requirements, borrowed from vision testing
Slide at 02:00 · click any slide to enlarge

Robustness requirements, borrowed from vision testing

  • Drawing an analogy to testing vision models under fog or camera shake, the speaker argues agent specs need explicit robustness ranges: how many typos before a customer-facing agent breaks, how much rephrasing it tolerates, and how stable results are under those variations -- benchmarks need to go beyond a static test set into task- and role-specific robustness testing.

“could typos disrupt it? How many typos disrupt it? Like how frustrated will people get? Rephrasing, how how stable under change are are the results?”

said at 06:50
05Using the spec for security testing and staying implementation-independent
Slide at 12:10 · click any slide to enlarge · 3 more from this section below

Using the spec for security testing and staying implementation-independent

  • SafeIntelligence pulls this spec information into security testing directly: knowing what an agent is supposed to do reveals where it's most vulnerable, since it will be willing to discuss its intended domains and has real permissions/power in the tasks it's built to perform.
  • The speaker recommends keeping specs and tests independent of the underlying agent implementation (e.g.
  • LangSmith, Vertex agents) so integration, unit, and penetration tests survive a later infrastructure switch.

“if you know what an agent is trying to do, you know the edges of where it's vulnerable because it's going to be willing to talk about those domains that it's supposed to act in”

said at 08:55

“Stay independent of the implementation cuz often you may, you know, may you may be building in LangSmith or something uh or Vertex agents or or or so on”

said at 10:57

Commentary (ours)

Treating the spec itself as the map of an agent's attack surface -- rather than deriving security tests from a separate red-teaming pass -- implies the two efforts (behavior spec and security testing) should share one artifact rather than live in separate teams' backlogs (ours).
