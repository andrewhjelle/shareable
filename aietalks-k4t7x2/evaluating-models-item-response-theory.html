Stop Evaluating Models Like It's the 50s

SpeakerAlejandro Vidal · Mindmakers Watchyoutu.be/O3FEoMYvUf8
Evals & benchmarksData curation

If you only read one thing

  • Classical test theory (counting right answers) treats every benchmark question as equally weighted, which Vidal calls an insane assumption once you look at item-level difficulty data.
  • On real data, Claude Opus 4.1 (245 right) and Gemini 3 Pro (247 right) look nearly tied by raw count, but item response theory shows almost a full standard deviation gap, meaning Gemini is meaningfully more capable.
  • Using item response theory to pick only the most discriminating items cut a 484-item benchmark to about 97 items (~5x smaller) while preserving 99% correlation with the original ranking — though well-designed benchmarks like GPQA don't compress this well.
01The problem with counting right answers

Vidal argues classical test theory (summing correct answers) implicitly assumes every benchmark question is equally important, which he calls an unreasonable assumption once you account for varying question difficulty and quality. He proposes item response theory (IRT), borrowed from psychometrics, as the fix: each item gets its own difficulty and discrimination parameters instead of being weighted equally.

“we are using a very strong assumption. We are saying that every question is equally important. They should weigh the same, which is kind of insane if you think about that.”

“GPT 5.5 here is going to be able to answer that questions because GPT 5 has a theta value of 1.2, and the difficulty of that item is minus 1.2. So, the probability of the right answer is 99.”

02A near-tie by raw score, a real gap by IRT

On a real benchmark, Claude Opus 4.1 scored 245 correct answers versus Gemini 3 Pro's 247 out of 337 questions, a small raw gap. Once scored through item response theory, the two models are separated by almost a full standard deviation, which Vidal interprets as Gemini being meaningfully more intelligent, not just marginally ahead.

“Cloud Opus 4.1, yeah? That has 245 right answers. On the other side we are going to have Gemini 3 Pro, which has 247”

▶ watch at 06:50

“if you use item response theory, you can see that the difference between all of them is almost one standard deviation. That means Gemini 3 Pro is by far more intelligent.”

▶ watch at 07:15
03Using IRT to audit and shrink benchmarks

Vidal shows that IRT lets you identify mislabeled or low-quality benchmark items (items where better models unexpectedly get the answer wrong) and either fix or remove them. He also demonstrates picking only the most discriminating items to shrink a benchmark from 484 items to about 97 while keeping 99% correlation with the full ranking, calling this roughly a 5x reduction in eval cost.

“you can actually uh pick the best items, the best questions on your benchmark, and remove the other ones that aren't working or even fix them.”

▶ watch at 08:20

“we're going to get that around 97 items compared with 484. That's almost 5x.”

▶ watch at 11:55
04Not every benchmark compresses the same way

Vidal notes GPQA is an exception: because its items are all highly discriminating and don't overlap in what they measure, random sampling of items produces about the same result as full evaluation, so it doesn't benefit from the same compression trick.

“GPQA, which is a extremely well-designed uh data set, the benchmark here, as you can see, even if you pick at random, you're going to get more or less the same result.”

▶ watch at 13:00
05Protecting benchmarks with adaptive testing

To prevent benchmark leakage, Vidal proposes splitting items into a shared 'anchor set' used across all evaluated organizations and a harder, organization-specific 'fingerprint set' shown only to one lab, letting residual analysis later detect whether a lab trained on the leaked fingerprint items.

“I'm going to pick uh one individual set. I'm going to call that fingerprint set that I'm going to show only to that specific organization.”
