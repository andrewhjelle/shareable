Stop Burning Tokens: Why self-improvement needs domain expertise first

SpeakerAnnabell Schäfer · Langfuse Watchyoutu.be/eAXxdtNlK04 · 17 min SourceRecording of this talk at AI Engineer (2026), published on YouTube by the conference. patternEvals & benchmarksContinual learningAgentic engineering

Gives a concrete recipe for building auto-improving agent loops: use domain experts to define binary pass/fail criteria instead of vague 1-5 quality scores, and cap the loop with a stopping condition

If you only read one thing

  • A self-optimization loop (Claude Opus proposing prompt edits) lifted a paper-classification agent from 68% to 83% accuracy on held-out data, with most of the gain from a single early iteration.
  • Warns that generic 0-1 or 1-5 scored evaluators like 'correctness' or 'helpfulness' are low-signal and inconsistent between runs because LLM-as-judge scoring is itself nondeterministic.
  • Recommends building high-signal binary (yes/no) evaluators sourced from domain experts, and giving optimization loops a stopping mechanism so they don't burn tokens without improving.

Their classification-loop experiment lifted accuracy from 68% to 83%

  • Langfuse ran a minimal self-optimization loop where an agent classified archive papers by label and Claude Opus 4.5 proposed prompt updates based on error analysis on a fit/validate/test split.
  • Accuracy rose from a 68% baseline to 83% on the fit/validate sets and generalized to 80.2% on a held-out 300-item test set, with most of the gain occurring in the first iteration.

03Generic scored evaluators are low-signal and inconsistent
Slide at 11:20 · click any slide to enlarge

Generic scored evaluators are low-signal and inconsistent

  • She warns that LLM-as-judge evaluation is itself nondeterministic, so re-running the same evaluator on the same output can produce a different score.
  • She argues common evaluators like 'correctness' or 'helpfulness' scored on a 0-1 or 1-5 scale are low-signal unless every point on the scale is explicitly defined, which is rarely done.

“there's barely any deterministic yes no target functions in most cases you will run it this time and next time you get a different”

said at 11:30

“for this to really work properly you need to define each of those numbers.”

said at 12:34
04Build binary, domain-specific evaluators instead
Slide at 11:20 · click any slide to enlarge

Build binary, domain-specific evaluators instead

  • Her recommendation is to replace vague scored criteria with binary yes/no checks tied to what the application actually needs to get right, such as whether a claimed fact is grounded in retrieved context.

“instead of correctness the answer is based on the knowledge base yes no.”

said at 13:04
05Get there by working with domain experts and adding a stopping condition
Slide at 14:50 · click any slide to enlarge · 2 more from this section below

Get there by working with domain experts and adding a stopping condition

  • She recommends building these evaluators together with domain experts, reviewing production data as a human rather than only through coding agents, and giving self-optimization loops an explicit stopping/escape condition so they don't keep running and burning tokens without improving.

“the most important thing we see is working with your experts”

said at 14:37

“giving the system an escape hatch instead of having it work for hours and hours uh hitting a wall and uh burning tokens.”
