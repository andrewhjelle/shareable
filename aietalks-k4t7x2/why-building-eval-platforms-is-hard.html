Why building eval platforms is hard

SpeakerPhil Hetzel · Braintrust Watchyoutu.be/_fQ7Z_Wfouk · 25 min SourceRecording of this talk at AI Engineer (2026), published on YouTube by the conference. Quotes are verbatim from the talk transcript with the time they were said.
takeEvals & benchmarksInfra & servingProduct & UX

Maps the maturity path a team goes through building or buying an eval platform (spreadsheet, vibe-coded UI, playground, production-trace flywheel) and names the specific data-systems failure modes -- 10-20MB traces, a hated custom query ...

If you only read one thing

  • Evals matter because LLMs have extreme output variability and agents are becoming the default way customers interact with companies, so unvalidated agents carry brand, compliance, and cost/maintenance risk in production.
  • Teams mature through recognizable stages: spreadsheet (documenting, not experimenting), vibe-coded UI (still reporting, not iterating), then a playground; the real inflection is connecting evals to production trace data in a continuous flywheel, since measuring agent quality is a systems problem, not just a UI/UX problem.
  • Agent traces break normal data infrastructure -- spans that would be a couple kilobytes in traditional apps run 10-20 megabytes here -- which pushed Braintrust to build custom infrastructure including a query DSL, BTQL, that Hetzel says the company itself hated.

01Why evals matter now

Why evals matter now

  • The speaker frames evals as necessary because LLMs have extreme output variability by design, and agents built on LLMs are becoming the default way customers interact with companies.
  • Without confidence in agent behavior before and after production, teams risk brand damage, compliance exposure, and ongoing cost and maintenance problems.

02Early stages: spreadsheets and vibe-coded UIs plateau fast

Early stages: spreadsheets and vibe-coded UIs plateau fast

  • The starting point most teams reach is a spreadsheet plus a for-loop: a way to execute the agent, a UI (even just the spreadsheet), and a way to gather input examples.
  • This has no barrier to entry but the speaker calls it documenting rather than experimenting, since it becomes cumbersome to compare experiments over time and hard to bring in non-technical domain experts.

“some way to execute your agent, some UI, sometimes it's as simple as a spreadsheet to show those outputs and scores, and then also a way to to gather input examples”

said at 06:44

“this is more I would call this documenting. It it's not really experimenting.”

03The flywheel: evals and observability are the same systems problem

The flywheel: evals and observability are the same systems problem

  • Braintrust started three years ago as an evals-only platform, then noticed a customer piping all of their production traffic into a database to run an hourly eval against it -- which pushed the company to build native tracing and observability.
  • The speaker's framing is that measuring agent quality, whether through evals or observability, is fundamentally a systems problem, not just a UI/UX problem.

“we used to be uh 3 years ago when we started we were only an evals platform. And then we noticed one of our customers was running this massive eval like a every hour of every day.”

said at 14:36

“measuring agent quality, performing evals, performing observability. It's actually a systems problem. It's not just a UI UX problem.”

said at 19:17
04Agent traces break traditional data infrastructure

Agent traces break traditional data infrastructure

  • Agent traces are unstructured, huge, and high-velocity: a traditional application trace span might be a couple of kilobytes, but the speaker has seen agent trace spans of 10-20 megabytes.
  • To support both low-latency trace viewing and aggregate analytics, Braintrust originally stitched together an open-source data warehouse, DuckDB in the browser, and a custom domain-specific query language called BTQL that, per the speaker, no one liked, including the team that built it.

“agent traces specifically, if you kind of look on the on the screen, these are really nasty. They're not like normal application traces.”

said at 17:12

“traditional span would be like a couple of kilobytes. Here, we've seen spans that are 10, 20 megabytes in size.”

“a domain-specific language that we created called BTQL that no one liked and and including us we we we hated it”

said at 18:46

Commentary (ours)

The BTQL admission is a useful data point for anyone weighing build-vs-buy on eval infrastructure: even a company whose product is evals ended up with a custom query layer it regretted, which suggests the data-systems cost of a homegrown platform is easy to underestimate (ours).
