Notion's Token Town

SpeakerSarah Sachs · Notion Watchyoutu.be/-I5W5QVAT8E · 23 min SourceRecording of this talk at AI Engineer (2026), published on YouTube by the conference. patternInfra & servingSecurityAgentic engineering

Sachs lays out concrete token-economics traps (locked-in providers, silent price hikes on model upgrades) that determine whether an AI product stays viable as usage scales

If you only read one thing

  • 88% of companies never get past 'AI as assistant' into reliable, system-level AI, largely due to siloed data and cost structure.
  • Being locked into one model provider removes your negotiating leverage; Notion's auto-model router handles about 75% of its traffic without committing to one lab.
  • The lethal trifecta (private data + untrusted content + external communication) is the next hard problem, ahead of raw capability.

01Most companies stall before real AI systems
Slide at 03:50 · click any slide to enlarge · 4 more from this section below

Most companies stall before real AI systems

  • Sachs frames the maturity curve as thought partner, then assistant, then teammate, then full system-of-record integration, and says almost no company reaches the last stage.
  • She attributes this to siloed data rather than model capability, arguing a durable system of record is the prerequisite for a working 'software factory.'

“88% of people can't even get past AI as an assistant.”

02Token pricing is a trap if you don't stay agnostic
Slide at 05:20 · click any slide to enlarge · 8 more from this section below

Token pricing is a trap if you don't stay agnostic

  • She warns that upgrading a model can silently raise real cost (more output tokens per call, or a flat price increase on a new model generation) without a matching increase in customer revenue.
  • Being tied to a single provider removes any ability to walk away from a bad deal, so she recommends staying model-agnostic and treating optionality itself as leverage in negotiations.

“your supplier is your competitor. I know very few people who have convinced me that that's not true.”

03Not every task needs the frontier model
Slide at 12:00 · click any slide to enlarge · 7 more from this section below

Not every task needs the frontier model

  • Sachs argues price does not track capability in the current oligopoly market, since a lab just behind the frontier only needs to undercut by about a dollar per million tokens to capture the rest of the market.
  • Notion routes roughly 75% of its traffic through an auto-model system rather than defaulting everything to the most expensive model, and she recommends offloading simple, deterministic work (CSV-to-PDF, SQL queries, tool calls) to CPU-based code instead of an LLM at all.

“It's the same with model pricing, which means that price does not correlate with capability growth”

said at 12:03

“we also have an auto model there at the top that handles about 75% of our traffic”

said at 13:05

“you don't need an LLM to turn a CSV into a PDF. You don't need an LLM to talk to notion tool calls if we have a CLI. You definitely don't need an LLM to do deterministic SQL queries.”

04Open weights as a negotiating lever
Slide at 15:00 · click any slide to enlarge · 8 more from this section below

Open weights as a negotiating lever

  • She positions open-weight models, now strong enough with RL post-training to cover tasks that used to require frontier-only models, as both a lower-cost option and as leverage that pressures the closed-lab oligopoly's pricing.

“openweight models are really strong enough to handle these tasks and the possibility to RL on top of them has also kind of expanded the upmarket growth that they can cover”

said at 15:07
05Security is the next open problem
Slide at 18:10 · click any slide to enlarge · 8 more from this section below

Security is the next open problem

  • Citing Simon Willison's 'lethal trifecta,' Sachs says any system combining private-data access, exposure to untrusted content, and the ability to communicate externally is exposing risk, and that risk scales with how autonomous and unsupervised the agent is.

“The second you have that system, you're exposing risk. And in fact, the more autonomous your system is, the more unsupervised this risk is.”
