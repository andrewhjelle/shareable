You Can't Prompt the Room: The Last Skill AI Won't Replace

SpeakerBalázs Horváth · VisualLabs Watchyoutu.be/6bmM45jkMDY
Product & UXAgentic engineering

If you only read one thing

  • At an internal hackathon, 17 of 21 agent ideas were abandoned because they created no business value; the 4 that succeeded came from properly scoping the problem first, not from better prompting.
  • Well-structured user stories (persona, need, why, with acceptance criteria) get better results from AI because AI was trained on that familiar structure -- daisy-chaining them into a story map produces the specification that becomes the code.
  • Anti-patterns to fix: tracking features shipped instead of features used more than twice, high login/trial numbers without repeat use, treating a demo as the deliverable, and writing a PRD with no real user testers.
01The bottleneck moved from writing code to eliciting requirements

The speaker frames the shift: getting access to code and being able to build is no longer the bottleneck in the software development lifecycle; the real bottleneck is getting stakeholders and decision-makers into the room to elicit the right requirements. He illustrates with an internal hackathon where 17 of 21 agent ideas were abandoned for lacking business value or data access, and only 4 had real impact.

“we had about 21 agents uh agent ideas and 17 of those were abandoned because they actually created no um business value”

02AI defaults to average outputs unless steered toward better requirements

Using Henry Ford's 'faster horses' analogy, he argues that using AI to build what already exists just replicates the common answer, since models are tuned to give the most common response; the job of the analyst/consultant is to move the output away from that average toward something meaningfully better.

“the chances are that you are replicating what already exists because AI by definition is coded to give you the most common answers”

▶ watch at 03:33
03Story mapping structures user stories for both AI and stakeholders

He recommends story mapping: a backbone of process stages (e.g., contacting, triaging, resolving, closing a case) with user stories underneath, kept high-level enough to give a big picture while prioritizing the first release (MVP) versus backlog items. Every user story should follow a persona/need/why structure with acceptance criteria, which he says AI handles well because it was trained on that well-known format.

“It is intended to stay at a fairly high level so you can get a a big picture, and then in you can decide what it is that you want to build and release”

▶ watch at 05:09

“AI is really good at pattern recognition and it was actually trained on the user story structure because it's a very well-known and well-used setup”

▶ watch at 06:41
04Four screening questions before building

Before building, he asks: whose problem is this (a specific named persona), what does winning look like for them, what would make them refuse to use it, and would it change a decision they make. He calls the overall thinking process VAD (value, architecture, design) -- understand how value is created, the process that supports it, then design the system around that value.

“as whose problem is this? Whose problem are we actually solving?”

▶ watch at 07:43
05Anti-patterns that signal you're building the wrong thing

Warning signs include high feature-shipping velocity with poor adoption, trial usage without repeat use, treating a demo as the finished deliverable, and PRDs written without real user testers. He recommends replacing 'number of features shipped last quarter' with 'number of features used more than twice' as a KPI, and moving subject matter experts into more customer-facing roles in the decision-making process.

“if the demo is the deliverable. We want to make sure that people can can put things into production”

▶ watch at 11:40

“the number of features shipped last quarter, that should be should be eliminated and you should just start looking at the number of features that we shipped that is actually used more than twice”

Commentary (ours)

The claim that 'AI is coded to give you the most common answers' is asserted as a fact about model training rather than demonstrated -- it's a reasonable intuition about mode-seeking behavior in RLHF-tuned models but the talk offers no evidence for it beyond the Ford analogy (ours).
