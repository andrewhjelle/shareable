Chat and citations won't save your vertical AI

SpeakerAtul Ramachandran · Filed Inc Watchyoutu.be/RGiXcVxSD3s · 15 min SourceRecording of this talk at AI Engineer (2026), published on YouTube by the conference. takeVertical AIProduct & UXAgentic engineering

Argues that chat plus citations, the default agent-product interface, structurally can't deliver the 'agent works while you sleep' promise because chat is synchronous and citations push verification work back onto the user

If you only read one thing

  • Chat is synchronous (the user waits for a response) and citations shift verification work back to the client, so neither delivers the 'AI works while you sleep' promise vertical AI products often sell.
  • Building for 'agentic delegation' requires four product elements: tasks worth delegating (over an hour of work), a way for users to train the agent on their own conventions, monitoring, and the ability to regain control, including a pre-approval plan for irreversible actions.
  • The right growth metric for a delegation product is weekly active sessions (work happening off-platform) rather than weekly active users, which should ideally decrease as trust in delegation increases.

01Chat and citations don't deliver the time-savings promise

Chat and citations don't deliver the time-savings promise

  • Vertical AI products typically sell customers on agents doing work while they sleep, using chat for input and citations for verifiable output.
  • The speaker argues chat's synchronous nature prevents users from leaving the platform while the agent works, and citations shift the burden of checking every claim back onto the client, which is especially costly in regulated verticals like healthcare, legal, and tax.

“quotes also shift the burden of verification back to the client. So consider reviewing the agent's work one by one now to make sure everything is correct.”

02Three eras of product abstraction end in agentic delegation
Slide at 04:50 · click any slide to enlarge · 3 more from this section below

Three eras of product abstraction end in agentic delegation

  • The speaker frames product history in three stages using banking as an example: physical branches (bottlenecked by employee count), digital self-service apps (bottlenecked by user count), and now agentic delegation, where users hand off long-running work to agents.
  • In this third stage the bottleneck on value created shifts away from how often a user visits the platform, since agents can work while the user is away.

“if a user comes into your product and starts delegating work, long-term work, what happens here is that the bottleneck, which is the number of users, also disappears”

said at 04:05
03Building the conveyor belt: delegation, training, monitoring, control
Slide at 06:50 · click any slide to enlarge · 5 more from this section below

Building the conveyor belt: delegation, training, monitoring, control

  • Building for delegation requires identifying tasks users currently spend more than an hour on and making them repeatable long-running background agent jobs.
  • A skills layer lets users train the agent on their own conventions to close the remaining 20% gap between a generic 80-90% output and what the user actually wants.
  • For irreversible or risky actions, such as entering data into tax software that could erase existing records, the product should present a plan for approval before acting, not act unilaterally.

“you, as a developer or product engineer, need to find tasks that your users perform that take more than a few hours”

said at 07:08

“This is the last 20% of the work you need to do. This is where the real value lies, the specifics of the job, you know, that you take into account.”

said at 09:11

“before entering data into our tax software, which may erase their existing data, we create a plan for them so they can approve it before moving forward”

04Measure weekly active sessions, not weekly active users
Slide at 13:00 · click any slide to enlarge

Measure weekly active sessions, not weekly active users

  • Because delegation-based products succeed when users trust the system enough to leave work running unattended, the speaker argues the standard weekly-active-users metric is the wrong signal.
  • He recommends tracking weekly active sessions, defined as tasks completed by a person or agent even when the user isn't on the platform, with weekly active users ideally decreasing (not to zero) as sessions increase.

“your actual goal should be to decrease the number of active users per week while increasing the number of active sessions per week, right?”

said at 13:49

Commentary (ours)

The weekly-active-sessions metric is easy to state but hard to instrument honestly -- a vendor could inflate session count by triggering agent runs that don't correspond to real user trust, so the metric needs a paired quality gate (something like the completion/approval rate per session) to avoid becoming its own vanity number (ours).
