Does GenAI "belong" to data scientists?

SpeakerPhil Hetzel · Braintrust Watchyoutu.be/NKwIX3CiRgU · 18 min SourceRecording of this talk at AI Engineer (2026), published on YouTube by the conference. takeAgentic engineeringEvals & benchmarksProduct & UX

Hetzel argues against defaulting generative AI ownership to the existing ML/data-science team, based on a year of watching customer teams build agents

If you only read one thing

  • Traditional enterprises default agent ownership to the existing ML/data-science platform team because 'generative AI has AI in the name,' while AI-native companies use small cross-functional teams instead (c1, c2).
  • Data scientists bring rigor around testing and understanding model risk, but often over-index on precision/recall/F1 metrics that don't capture an agent's much broader functional surface area (c4, c5).
  • Hetzel's answer is neither: build diverse teams where non-technical subject-matter experts control prompts and do human annotation (closest proximity to the problem), while data scientists own guardrails, LLM-as-judge evals, and fine-tuning (c6, c7).

Two org patterns for who gets handed agent work

  • Hetzel describes traditional enterprises where a CEO/CIO reads about agents and delegates the mandate down to the existing ML/data-science platform team, versus AI-native companies that never had a legacy ML team and instead use small, cross-functional groups spanning product, engineering, and AI.
  • He notes AI natives' smaller size gives each person closer proximity to the actual problem being solved.

02What's actually different from traditional ML
Slide at 06:40 · click any slide to enlarge · 2 more from this section below

What's actually different from traditional ML

  • Unlike traditional ML, the underlying model is already trained by Anthropic, OpenAI, or Mistral, so teams don't run a training/testing pipeline -- they still must run evals after implementing the APIs.
  • Behavior changes come from adjusting prompts and context rather than retraining or feature engineering, which opens the door to people without a stats background.

“we still need to as as uh AI teams, we still need to perform evals after we've implemented uh those APIs in into our product”

said at 06:28
03The case for data scientists owning it, and the case against
Slide at 07:50 · click any slide to enlarge

The case for data scientists owning it, and the case against

  • In favor: data scientists understand how LLMs work, have rigorous processes for pushing models to production, and bring a rigorous testing mindset.
  • Against: models are already built so there's no training/cross-validation pipeline, and Hetzel's biggest concern is that ML engineers often fixate on precision/recall/F1 metrics that don't capture the much broader functional surface area agents need to be evaluated against.

“they're going to have a far better appreciation of the risks inherent with using this very complex technology”

said at 08:00

“they will really lock on to the traditional ML engineer metrics like precision, recall, F1, um and they'll obsess over those metrics because that is what has gotten them there up to that point”

04The case for non-data-scientists
Slide at 11:50 · click any slide to enlarge · 2 more from this section below

The case for non-data-scientists

  • LLMs are just APIs, which is core product-engineering territory, and complex distributed agents (a supervisor calling sub-agents across different infrastructure) are a systems problem more than a stats problem.
  • Non-technical subject-matter experts and product managers have the closest proximity to the problem the agent solves and add value by controlling prompts and doing human annotation on agent traces.

“these people are the ones that have the most proximity to the problem that the agent is trying to solve”

said at 11:16
05Hetzel's synthesis: diverse teams, not ownership
Slide at 12:20 · click any slide to enlarge · 8 more from this section below

Hetzel's synthesis: diverse teams, not ownership

  • Hetzel lands on building diverse teams rather than assigning agent ownership to one function: data scientists add guardrails, own LLM-as-judge eval design, and handle fine-tuning when needed; non-technical experts do human annotation and prompt/context engineering; product/systems engineers implement the surrounding infrastructure and production feedback loops.

“I think data scientists can can be the adult in the room uh during those situations and say, you know, the the LLM, this is how it's trained”

said at 13:27

Commentary (ours)

Hetzel's framing implicitly argues against a title-based ownership model in favor of a proximity-based one -- whoever is closest to the failure mode should control the lever that fixes it, whether that's a prompt, a guardrail, or a fine-tune (ours).
