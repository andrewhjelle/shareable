The Era of Compound Engineering

This talk lays out a specific operating loop and time-allocation rule (spend half of every task teaching the system, not just shipping the feature) for a solo engineer running a production product with AI agents

If you only read one thing

  • Kieran runs Kora, a production AI email client, largely alone; he says he hasn't written a line of code this year and started the current rebuild in January.
  • His loop (brainstorm, plan, work, review, polish, compound) puts 50% of effort into teaching the system after each task, on the claim that one engineer with a compounding system beats full teams using AI without it.
  • His compound engineering plugin is used by hundreds of thousands of people daily; his bet is that implementation keeps getting cheaper while judgment does not.

The loop: brainstorm, plan, work, review, polish, compound

  • Kieran describes a repeating loop he calls the human-AI sandwich: humans provide judgment at the brainstorming start and the raise-the-bar polish at the end, with AI doing the work in between.
  • He claims one engineer running this compounding loop beats full teams that use AI without a system for capturing what was learned.

03Why extraction beats repetition

Why extraction beats repetition

  • His core rule is to spend roughly half of every task teaching the system anything it got wrong, not just shipping the feature, storing that knowledge as solution documents in his repository rather than in chat history.
  • He argues this is more token-efficient long term because the system already has the right answers embedded, cutting the need for review or repeated research.

“make sure to extract never repeat if you see yourself repeating yourself make sure to extract it somehow”

said at 16:37

“in my research it's actually more token efficient because if you have the right answers and the right solutions already within the token you don't need to do review”

said at 08:19

The bet: implementation cheapens, judgment doesn't

  • Kieran's closing framing is that implementation is only getting cheaper while judgment is not, so future systems need access to accumulated judgment and taste to keep delivering leverage, which is why he treats the compounding-knowledge step as the real bottleneck to invest in.

Commentary (ours)

The team-beating claim (c8) is presented without a benchmark or comparison group, so it reads as Kieran's own operating conviction rather than a measured result; useful as a design principle to test rather than a proven outcome.
