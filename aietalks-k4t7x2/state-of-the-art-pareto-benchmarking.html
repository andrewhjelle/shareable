20 days of compute vs 7 hours: rethinking what state-of-the-art means

SpeakerBertrand Charpentier · Pruna Watchyoutu.be/hqHC6Z_lXyo · 19 min SourceRecording of this talk at AI Engineer (2026), published on YouTube by the conference. Quotes are verbatim from the talk transcript with the time they were said.
patternEvals & benchmarksInfra & servingGenerative media

Shows public leaderboards for image/video generation models disagree with each other and with internal automated metrics, and that reproducing a leaderboard-scale evaluation can cost 20 days of compute and roughly 400 marathons of energy

If you only read one thing

  • Different public leaderboards (LM Arena, Design Arena, Artificial Analysis) rank the same image-editing models differently, and their Elo score ranges don't even align (c1, c2).
  • No model wins close to 100% of head-to-head battles, so picking the top-ranked model still means the wrong model for roughly 40% of a given use case (c3).
  • Reproducing a 26K-battle leaderboard evaluation with a full-size model took 20 days of compute and 5K dollars, versus 7 hours and 265 dollars with a smaller model Pruna built for the same evaluation (c5, c6).

Public leaderboards disagree with each other

  • Comparing LM Arena (Arena), Design Arena, and Artificial Analysis on image-editing models shows different top ranks, different relative orderings between models, and models that appear on some leaderboards but not others.
  • The Elo scores meant to represent model quality also use inconsistent ranges across leaderboards, for example 1,100-1,300 on one versus a completely different range on another, making it hard to tell how strong models really are relative to each other.

02No leaderboard model wins consistently, and manual inspection is biased

No leaderboard model wins consistently, and manual inspection is biased

  • Even the best-ranked models lose at least 40% of their head-to-head battles, so if a use case falls in that 40% the top-ranked model is the wrong choice.
  • Manual inspection of a handful of generated images is also unreliable: the room's own preferences split across images, showing evaluators are biased both by their personal taste and by the small number of samples they happen to look at.

“there are no models which are close to 100% win rates. It means that most of the models they lose at least 40% of their of their battles.”

“you are super biased toward the few samples that you look at. So, when you do manual inspections, you are two times biased by you and by also the number of samples the specific samples you you look at.”

03Reproducing a leaderboard evaluation is expensive
Slide at 12:40 · click any slide to enlarge · 2 more from this section below

Reproducing a leaderboard evaluation is expensive

  • The speaker recreated the compute cost behind a 26K-battle leaderboard evaluation: at roughly 62 seconds per image, running it with a full-size model took 20 days of compute, cost about 5K dollars, and consumed enough energy to equal roughly 400 marathons.
  • Running the same 26K-evaluation set with a smaller model built for sub-1-second generation took 7 hours and 265 dollars instead.

“in total to do this 26 K evaluations, it takes 20 days of compute. In terms of cost, it's 5 K just 5 K just to to to run this evaluation.”

said at 12:50

“it takes only 7 hours. It takes also like it uses also way less money, so 265 dollars.”

04There is no single state of the art, only a Pareto frontier
Slide at 14:20 · click any slide to enlarge · 8 more from this section below

There is no single state of the art, only a Pareto frontier

  • Rather than one best model, plotting quality against an efficiency metric like latency or price produces a Pareto front with several models on it, since quality scores can be nearly flat across models while efficiency differs by an order of magnitude.
  • The same approach works for a specific use case, such as text rendering, by swapping in a use-case-specific quality metric instead of a general one.

“to the question what model is state of the art, the answer is there are multiple state of the art model. And the tool I prefer for this is usually the Pareto plots”

said at 14:22

“there is a big difference in terms of efficiency. So, you can be like really like times times I don't know, 20 times faster just by using the different”

said at 14:52

Commentary (ours)

The core move here is treating evaluation itself as a cost center with its own compute budget, not a free side-step before deployment; that framing implies teams should be sizing evaluation spend against the marginal quality gain it can actually surface, the same way they'd size a training run (ours).
