How Evals and Prompts Shape Agent Behavior

SpeakerPreetika Bhateja & Daniel Bump · YouTube Ads Watchyoutu.be/xyL2Ltkh-SA
Evals & benchmarksAgentic engineering

If you only read one thing

  • Optimize the agent's tool foundation before building comprehensive evals, and let early unscalable 'vibing' run before formal scaled rating, since jumping to scaled raters too early causes noisy swings while you're still calibrating (c1, c2, c3)
  • Evals should test negatives (did the agent avoid doing something bad), not just positive task completion, and raters need clear rubrics plus explanations of their ratings to be useful (c4, c5)
  • One agent kept removing legally required disclaimers from ads despite explicit instructions not to -- caught only by reading traces, not by pass/fail rates -- which reinforces focusing fixes on patterns across a golden set rather than isolated examples, with a clear precision/recall-based launch gate (c6, c7, c8)
01Optimize tools before scaling evals

Before building larger agent evals, the team recommends first optimizing the agent's set of LLM-friendly tools and, where gaps remain, adding an independent critique agent with a remediation loop for self-correction.

“it's important to first optimize these tools and make sure they're the best they can be before just jumping onto um larger agent evals”

▶ watch at 01:33
02Vibing is a legitimate early stage

Rather than jumping straight to a comprehensive, scalable eval, the team found it more effective to start with an intuition-based, non-scalable approach: eyeballing outputs to quickly identify failure patterns and iterate on radical prompt or architecture changes. Moving to scaled raters too early, before the model and eval are both stable, produces large, confusing swings that are hard to interpret.

“early on vibing can actually be kind of good for you”

▶ watch at 03:38

“kind of have like very big ups and downs as you might be iterating and calibrating the eval as you are struggling with uh changing the model radically.”

▶ watch at 05:45
03Test the negatives, not just the positives

Starting small with a few core tasks and a small golden set, the team emphasizes that checking whether an agent avoided doing something bad is just as important as checking whether it completed the intended task.

“Checking if the model like didn't do something as bad, uh, something bad is just as critical as checking if it did the task.”

▶ watch at 06:15
04Getting good signal from raters

Working with scale raters, the team found that giving clear rubrics with explicit examples reduces ambiguity, and that collecting explanations for why a rater passed or failed something (not just the pass/fail label) surfaces where the agent actually needs to improve, especially for multi-axis evaluations like brand safety versus accuracy.

“So it's helpful to get explanations of why they're rating something a certain way.”

▶ watch at 08:20
05Failure example: removing a required disclaimer

Despite being explicitly instructed multiple times never to remove legally required disclaimers, the agent was found in some edge cases to detect a disclaimer and remove it anyway, in direct contradiction of its instructions. This was only caught by reading the agent's reasoning traces, not by aggregate pass/fail rates.

“it says, okay, I found a disclaimer and now I'm going to go ahead and remove it, which was not what we asked it to do.”

▶ watch at 11:28

Commentary (ours)

(ours) The disclaimer-removal failure is a good illustration of why aggregate pass-rate metrics can hide instruction-following failures that only appear in a minority of traces; it's the same argument the Snorkel and Uber talks make from different angles about needing to inspect traces, not just scores.
