If you only read one thing

  • Full-duplex voice conversation needs roughly 200ms latency or less to feel natural, which is very hard to hit through a full network-plus-inference chain; voice-in/visuals-out is more forgiving because visual responses within about a second still feel seamless.
  • GPT-5 mini, despite being smaller and cheaper, showed 5,000-10,000ms P95 latency in production, while a Haiku-class model hit the needed responsiveness -- model size alone doesn't predict real-time usability.
  • Three levers for staying in the latency envelope: use a fast (Haiku-class) model with a short context, send inference eagerly every 1-2 seconds rather than waiting for silence, and keep a stable prefix so caching can deliver up to 90% cheaper, faster inference.

Karpathy's voice-in, visuals-out framing

  • The talk opens from Andrej Karpathy's argument that voice is the human-preferred input for AI while visuals are the preferred output, contrasted with the typing-in/typing-out interaction most AI products still use.
  • The speaker notes visuals-out has become far more capable recently, with models generating rich HTML, interactive controls, and illustrations, while voice-in remains the more controversial and harder half of the argument given people's frustrating experiences with existing voice assistants.

Latency budgets: 100ms, 1000ms, and the much harder 200ms

  • General product responsiveness has been understood since the 1960s to need about 100 milliseconds to feel instant, with a practical ceiling of around 1000 milliseconds before people lose their train of thought.
  • Full-duplex voice conversation is far more demanding, needing roughly 200 milliseconds of latency or less to feel like a natural back-and-forth, which is very difficult across a full network request, speech-to-text, model inference, and response chain.

“instant, it needs to react in about 100 milliseconds, a tenth of a second. And so of course we are always aspiring to get our products to react that fast”

said at 05:44

“But if we want to have a seamless voice conversation with it an AI or another human, the limit is much more aggressive”

said at 06:16
03Model choice matters more than model size
Slide at 09:30 · click any slide to enlarge · 6 more from this section below

Model choice matters more than model size

  • The team saw GPT-5 mini return latencies of 5,000-10,000 milliseconds (P95 around 7,000ms) despite being a smaller, cheaper model, and it never consistently responded fast enough for their real-time use case.
  • A Haiku-class model performed much better on P95 latency, and their architecture routes to a larger model asynchronously only when heavier reasoning is needed.

“were seeing latencies of 5,000 milliseconds, 7,000 P95, sometimes 10,000 millisecond latency for this small model. It was cheaper than the bigger model, but we weren't consistently or even ever seeing it respond fast enough”

said at 09:21
04Send inference eagerly and keep a stable cache prefix
Slide at 10:50 · click any slide to enlarge · 7 more from this section below

Send inference eagerly and keep a stable cache prefix

  • Rather than waiting for a pause in speech before running inference, the team sends inference eagerly every 1-2 seconds as the person talks, even without certainty that they've finished.
  • They also keep the first roughly 90% of the context window stable across requests to take advantage of prefix caching, which can deliver up to 90% cheaper, faster inference.

“when we want to have our inference pretty eagerly responding as the person is talking, even if we're not entirely sure they've stopped talking, being willing to send inference every 1 or 2 seconds as they speak”

said at 10:53

“then you can get up to 90% cheaper, faster inference um depending on the conditions. And so, you need to lean heavily into this architecture really I think for for most applications we're moving towards this”

said at 11:55

Commentary (ours)

The reframe of voice-in/visuals-out as a way to dodge the 200ms full-duplex latency wall rather than solve it is a pragmatic product decision, but it implicitly concedes that true conversational voice interfaces still require novel low-latency architectures (like the time-sliced approach mentioned from Thinking Machines) that most teams can't build themselves yet (ours).
