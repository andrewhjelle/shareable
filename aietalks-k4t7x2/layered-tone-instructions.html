Stop Writing Tone Instructions. Layer Them.

Gives a concrete four-layer prompt architecture (immutable identity, situational mode, example-anchored voice, post-generation veto) for anyone shipping a customer-facing brand voice agent, with a specific failure mode (offering booking ...

If you only read one thing

  • Single system prompts fail on unanticipated turns because one prompt is being asked to do four different jobs at once: hard rules, situational context, tone/voice, and self-checking.
  • The fix is four ordered layers -- immutable identity, situational mode, example-anchored voice, post-generation veto -- assembled through one canonical entry point instead of scattered ad hoc prompts.
  • The veto layer is the only deterministic one: it was added after the model kept confidently offering the couple booking dates that were already taken, since the calendar was never in its context.
▣The system in one picture (ours, from the slides and the transcript)

The four layers run in a fixed order, and only the last one is deterministic enough to catch bugs like phantom booking dates or a silently defaulted identity (ours).

Why one system prompt breaks on turn 21

  • The speaker frames the model as a brilliant intern with a terrible EQ: perfect recall of instructions but no instinct for when to deviate.
  • Standard advice (write a detailed brand-voice system prompt with examples) works for the 'happy path' of anticipated questions, but breaks on the first unanticipated turn because a single prompt is being asked to simultaneously be situational, expressive, inviolable, and self-checking.

02Layer 1: immutable identity
Slide at 05:10 · click any slide to enlarge · 6 more from this section below

Layer 1: immutable identity

  • The first layer holds hard rules the brand structurally cannot break, which cannot be overridden by venue config, voice profile, or user instruction.
  • Example: the AI must disclose it is an AI in its very first response, before being asked, as a trust bet rather than a legal requirement; a second example forbids language implying physical presence (e.g. offering to give a tour in person).

“you must confirm that in your very next message. Clearly and unambiguously confirm you are an AI assistant”

said at 05:05

“it is always forbidden to say, "I'd love to show you around." or "I can't wait to meet you in person."”

03Layer 2: situational mode

Layer 2: situational mode

  • This layer injects real-time context before generation: who the user is (coordinator vs. couple gets different tone) and what they're going through (soft context notes for tone/empathy, never quoted verbatim).
  • The assembler deliberately renders the human-context notes before numeric constraints so tone is set first.

“Tone is set by human context first, then numeric constraints. The code comment explains the reasoning”

said at 10:19
04Layer 3 is where most teams stop, and why that's not enough
Slide at 12:30 · click any slide to enlarge · 3 more from this section below

Layer 3 is where most teams stop, and why that's not enough

  • Layer 3 is the example-anchored tone guide -- the layer most engineering teams treat as the whole solution because it feels like a brand/marketing problem rather than a technical one.
  • It teaches the model what good looks like on the happy path but can't enforce hard rules, can't respond to situational context, and can't catch a bad output after the fact.

“But cannot enforce a rule that came out. That's layer one. It cannot respond to who is this person and what they're going through”

said at 12:21
05Layer 4: the post-generation veto, and the date-offering bug that motivated it
Slide at 14:20 · click any slide to enlarge · 1 more from this section below

Layer 4: the post-generation veto, and the date-offering bug that motivated it

  • Layer 4 is the only non-prompt layer: a deterministic, automated check that reads the actual output and can reject it, split into soft flags (an honesty inspector checking things like whether the response actually answered the question) and hard rejects (a numbers guard).
  • It was added after a recurring failure where the model, never given the calendar, would warmly offer a couple a wedding date that was already booked.

“A false positive means someone double-checked a phone response. A false negative remains a hallucinated number or a privacy violation that ships to a client”

said at 13:54

“The AI would keep offering dates to my clients that did not exist. A couple writes in excited about a Saturday in October”

said at 14:24
06Multi-tenant identity must never default silently
Slide at 17:20 · click any slide to enlarge · 6 more from this section below

Multi-tenant identity must never default silently

  • The same four-layer architecture serves multiple products/tenants (a wedding venue and a missing-persons tool, Thread Light) with layer 1 shared and layers 2-3 per-tenant.
  • A real production bug let venue identity default silently, causing every venue's AI to email from a stranger's address; the fix was to make missing brand identity throw an error rather than fall back.

“Letting them default silently caused every venue to ship as sage@hawthornemanner.com, which was a critical white label leak”

said at 17:02

Commentary (ours)

The load-bearing move here isn't the four-layer taxonomy itself but the claim that only the last layer is deterministic -- everything upstream of it is 'asking nicely.' That reframes prompt engineering as necessary but insufficient for anything a user can act on (dates, prices, promises), and argues for a cheap rules-based gate as a permanent fixture rather than a stopgap until models get better (ours).
