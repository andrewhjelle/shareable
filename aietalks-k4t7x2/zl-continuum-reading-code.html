Should AI Engineers Still Read Code in 2026? The Z/L Continuum

SpeakerAlex Volkov · ThursdAI Watchyoutu.be/ZpK5PWX2YRM
Coding agentsAgentic engineeringEvals & benchmarks

If you only read one thing

  • A 22,000-engineer Faros AI survey found 861% more code deletion per PR alongside 31% more PRs merged with zero review and 242% more incidents per PR (c4, c6, c7).
  • The right question isn't whether to read code, it's what proof each change needs: read every line of auth/money/permissions/irreversible-data, let agents decompose and self-verify the rest (c8).
  • Capability drift keeps moving where review happens -- from reading every line, to directing agents to the right code, to (soon) inspecting the loops that grade their own work (c9, c10).
01AI-written code volume exploded after late 2025

The speaker cites METR data showing models, for the first time, completing tasks that would take engineers over 16 hours, and dates a step-change in AI engineering to around December 2025. He reports that 80% of Anthropic's code is now AI-written (a stat he calls a few months stale, likely higher now), and that GitHub is on track for roughly 14x the commit volume of 2025, most of it AI-assisted.

“It shows that models, for the first time, started completing tasks that would take engineers over 16 hours to do.”

02Output is up, but so are incidents and unreviewed merges

Drawing on an April 2026 Faros AI survey of 22,000 engineers (the 'acceleration whiplash' report), the talk lists an 861% increase in code deletion per PR and Anthropic shipping eight times more code per quarter than in 2025. The same survey found a 31% increase in PRs merged with no review at all (human or agentic), a 242% increase in incidents per PR, and bugs per developer up six times versus 2025.

03Reframe: route review by task, not by person

The speaker argues the two conference speakers he built the continuum around (Ryan LeFebvre of OpenAI and Mario Zechner) agree more than they appear to: LeFebvre wants humans to inspect the system that catches repeated mistakes rather than every line, while Zechner wants every line read only for critical code. The resulting rule: read every line of authentication, money movement, permissions, and irreversible data; decompose everything else into atomic PRs and let agents verify, trace, eval, and shadow-mode test.

“You read every line of authentication, money movement, permissions, and irreversible data.”

▶ watch at 14:20
04Where the review layer moves next

Andrej Karpathy is quoted saying it has never felt so tempting to stop looking at code at all, but warning not to do that in production. The speaker frames this as capability drift: the review layer keeps moving up a level, from reading code, to directing agents toward the right proof, to -- with the rise of self-looping agents that write, run, and grade their own work -- inspecting the loops themselves.

“It's never felt so tempting to stop looking at code at all. But don't do this in production.”

▶ watch at 16:25

“If yesterday we inspected the outputs and we read the code, and today we inspect the task direction and kind of like directed to the right proof, maybe tomorrow we're inspecting the loops.”

▶ watch at 17:27

Commentary (ours)

The framing quietly assumes review capacity scales down as capability moves up a layer, but the survey stats he cites (more incidents, more bugs, more unreviewed merges) are evidence of the opposite happening in practice right now -- the routing table is a prescription for where review should go, not proof that teams are actually doing it (ours).
