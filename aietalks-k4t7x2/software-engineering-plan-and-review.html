Software Engineering Is Becoming Plan and Review

SpeakerLouis Knight-Webb · Vibe Kanban Watchyoutu.be/W76woOYHlvY

01Coding time didn't vanish, it moved to planning and review

Knight-Webb argues that as coding agents from GitHub Copilot through Claude Code have taken over writing code, the hours previously spent typing didn't come back as free time -- they shifted into planning and reviewing the agent's output. He frames it as an accelerant rather than a time refund: roughly 20 minutes gained for every 30 minutes previously spent coding, with the rest going to the new plan/review work.

“displaced work. Work that you or time that was previously spent doing the coding has moved. It has moved to planning and reviewing.”

02When to plan heavily vs. stay in the loop

He distinguishes a plan-based approach (comprehensive plan docs, spec frameworks, models asking clarifying questions up front) from a YOLO/review-heavy approach, arguing the plan-based approach costs more time up front but saves more time overall. His condensed takeaway: spending 5 minutes planning saves 30 minutes of reviewing AI-generated code, and he recommends being plan-heavy for back-end feature work, refactors, and migrations, while staying in the loop for stateful front-end feature work.

“spending 5 minutes of planning saves you 30 minutes of reviewing AI-generated code”

03Agent run times are stretching from seconds to minutes

He traces run times growing from GitHub Copilot's single-line completions, to the original Cursor completing a file in about 30 seconds, to Claude Code running for a minute or two last year and now producing good results with 5-10 minute executions this year, driven by agents doing more (type-checking, testing) before returning control. He predicts that within about 9 months, AI will start to be able to QA front-end work itself, calling it the next major breakthrough, though he notes he hasn't met anyone using this in mainstream development yet.

“run for maybe a minute or two, and this year I've I've been, you know, getting some pretty good results with 5- or 10-minute executions.”

04Crossing the 5-minute mark forces new work habits

He identifies roughly 5 minutes as the threshold past which a developer can no longer just sit and watch agent logs, requiring a behavioral shift once average run times exceed it. His proposed solution is parallelism -- running multiple agent workspaces at once so that finishing a review of one piece of work coincides with another agent finishing -- which is the premise behind Vibe Kanban's sidebar of multiple concurrent agent workspaces.

“5 minutes is roughly the time when you can like sit there and wait for something, watch the logs, probably more realistically like browse Twitter, something like that.”

“you run multiple of these things at once. Say each of them take 10 minutes to run. So, the way you get around the waiting problem is you have multiple of them on the go at any given time.”

05Vibe Kanban is shutting down

Knight-Webb announced live on stage that he decided on the preceding Tuesday to shut down Vibe Kanban, despite reaching 30,000 monthly active users and 25,000 GitHub stars (the project continues non-commercially). He attributes the shutdown to unit economics: unlike companies making money by selling to enterprise or reselling tokens, Vibe Kanban's ~$30 subscriptions were funding users who would then spend roughly $3,000 with Codex through the product, which he says wasn't sustainable.

“on Tuesday, I decided to shut the company down.”
