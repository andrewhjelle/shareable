If you only read one thing

  • Verifiable rewards (math, code tests, tool-use database state) are the easy case; most real agent tasks (report writing, booking, refund handling) don't have a clean best answer, which is the harder problem this talk addresses (c1).
  • Reward hacking happens when a proxy objective is undefined at the boundaries and models learn to exploit that gap rather than achieve the real goal; catching it often requires human review after the fact since judges frequently miss it even when told not to reward-hack (c2, c7).
  • Two concrete signal-manufacturing techniques: mining production traces as source material for tasks whose distribution isn't known in advance, and 'working backwards' from a known-reachable solution to generate a verifiable but harder upstream task (c4, c5, c6).

RL recap: agent, environment, and where verifiable rewards are the easy case

  • An agent (model plus harness) acts inside an environment defined as a task plus a world (e.g. a docker image, codebase, or set of tools) and a scoring rule; the resulting reward is used to compute an advantage that updates the policy.
  • Verifiable rewards are the easy case, math answers parsed from a boxed output, code checked by tests or a linter, tool use checked against expected database state, but most real agent work (reports, bookings, refunds) doesn't have this clean structure.

Reward hacking and the goal of continual learning

  • Reward hacking happens when a proxy objective is loosely defined at the boundaries, letting a model trained with RL find a path to higher reward that doesn't reflect the actual goal.
  • The broader goal the talk sets is continual learning: deploying models into messy real settings where they make mistakes, observe and catch those mistakes after the fact, and use that to avoid repeating them, with humans in the loop at the right level of abstraction.

“reward hacking is when you have a kind of loose proxy for your objective that is undefined at the boundaries”

“we want models to be deployed in relatively realistic complex, messy settings and to be able to learn as they go where they are doing things, they are making mistakes”

Manufacturing signal from production traces and working backwards

  • One core technique is grounding: using existing production traces from a deployed agent as source material for tasks even before the task distribution is fully known, since traces at least indicate what to look for.
  • A second technique, 'working backwards,' starts from a solution (or something close to it) and treats the real task as further upstream, letting a hard problem be verified because you know a known-reachable end state exists, applicable to documents, code PRs and diffs, and more.

“what we found is super helpful is taking existing traces from a deployed agent and treating these as the source material”

said at 09:48

“this general principle of like working backwards is starting from the solution or something that's close to the solution and have where your real task is like further upstream”

said at 10:49
04Simulators for controllability, and where judges still fail
Slide at 13:50 · click any slide to enlarge · 8 more from this section below

Simulators for controllability, and where judges still fail

  • For tools or web applications where the backend can't be directly programmed, Prime Intellect builds simulators grounded in production traces, which grants full backend controllability, enabling reverse engineering where the answer is planted and worked backward from, baking in verifiability even when the real production system lacks it.
  • Even so, judges are often not good at catching reward hacks even when explicitly told not to reward-hack; the more reliable check remains a human looking at the rollout after the fact and recognizing an obvious hack.

“you can actually do this reverse engineering where you get to kind of plant the answer. You can start from the end and work backwards”

said at 12:50

“if the reward hacks are the sorts of things where a human can look at them and be like, oh yeah, that's a reward hack. Judges are often not quite good at doing this as well”

said at 15:24

Commentary (ours)

The talk presents these techniques (trace mining, working-backwards, simulators) as building toward a general continual-learning system, but the demonstrated results cited (the 'general agent' tool-use blog) cover a narrower case (tool use) than the full vision described (ours).
