Don't Let the LLM Drive

SpeakerOrnella Bahidika & Joel Allou · Microsoft Watchyoutu.be/m24UKZomm7k · 6 min SourceRecording of this talk at AI Engineer (2026), published on YouTube by the conference. patternAgentic engineeringProduct & UX

Describes the harness pattern behind Ace, a live AI voice tutor: the LLM only executes one step of an externally-defined state machine at a time and never tracks lesson progress itself

If you only read one thing

  • Multi-step agent unreliability (skipping steps, looping, declaring done early) is framed as a control problem, not a prompting problem, so the fix is a harness/state-machine, not better prompts (c1, c2).
  • Because each step is tightly scoped by the harness, Ace runs on Haiku 4.5 rather than a larger model like Opus 4.7 and still performs at the expected level, saving cost, time, and latency (c3).
  • Ace's harness is organized around three questions engineered outside the model: is the lesson done, did the student get it right, and what comes next (c4).

01Reliability is a control problem
Slide at 00:40 · click any slide to enlarge

Reliability is a control problem

  • The speakers describe the common failure mode of a multi-step agent that works in demos but drifts, skips a step, or loops once a real user is on it, and argue the usual fix of adding more prompt instructions doesn't address the actual cause.

“reliability was never a prompting problem. It's a control problem”

said at 00:32
02Model as talent, harness as director
Slide at 01:20 · click any slide to enlarge · 1 more from this section below

Model as talent, harness as director

  • Ace is structured as a small state machine (intro, teach, check, grade, advance, wrap) where each step sends the model a narrow contract to execute one action and return it; the harness validates the response, advances state, and decides what happens next.
  • The model itself never tracks or decides which step the lesson is on.

“The model is the talent, and the harness is the director”

said at 00:35
03A smaller model performs at the expected level
Slide at 03:50 · click any slide to enlarge

A smaller model performs at the expected level

  • Because each step confines the model to a specific, scoped action rather than asking it to reason through the whole lesson, Ace runs on Haiku 4.5 instead of a larger model like Opus 4.7, and the harnessing around it lets it perform at the needed level while saving money, time, and latency.

“because of the harnessing around it, it's still able to perform at the level in which we expect, saving money, saving time, and saving latency”

said at 02:40
04Three questions engineered outside the model
Slide at 04:20 · click any slide to enlarge

Three questions engineered outside the model

  • For Ace specifically, the team engineered three categories of logic outside the model entirely: whether the lesson is done, whether the student actually got the material right, and what should happen next, with the model only receiving the resulting instruction as input.

“there are three things that we wanted to think about. Like, when is the lesson done is one, right? Did the student actually get it right?”

said at 04:15
05When to apply the pattern
Slide at 05:50 · click any slide to enlarge · 1 more from this section below

When to apply the pattern

  • The speakers generalize the pattern to coding agents, ops runbooks, and onboarding flows, and give a rule of thumb: if an agent's reliability is close to a coin flip, take control flow out of the model and build the decision logic around it instead.

“If it's somewhat of a coin flip, then you want to take the control flow out of the model”

said at 05:15

Commentary (ours)

(ours) This is a companion talk to their other lightning talk on model sizing for Ace; together they argue the harness-first approach is what makes the smaller-model choice viable, not the reverse.
