From Tokens to Cells: Foundation Models for Single-Cell Biology

SpeakerAkram Baharlouei · Altos Labs Watchyoutu.be/-561cZmir5Q
Healthcare AIVertical AIEvals & benchmarks

If you only read one thing

  • NeurIPS benchmarking last year found transformer-based single-cell foundation models sometimes underperform simple linear models despite far higher training cost.
  • Flow-matching models like PrimeFlow do better than transformer/autoencoder approaches because they match the data distribution rather than compressing to a latent vector and predicting a mean.
  • RNA-seq is the dominant training modality because it's the easiest to scale (tens of millions to ~1 billion cells), but scaling data alone won't fix the field without also improving data quality and realism.
01Why single-cell biology matters

The speaker frames single-cell modeling around Yamanaka's 2006 discovery that four transcription factors can reprogram an aged skin cell to an embryonic-stem-cell-like state, which won a Nobel Prize in 2012 and underlies partial reprogramming approaches aimed at restoring youthful cell function without changing cell type. He notes the first such reprogramming medicine (OSK) is being tested in humans this year, roughly 20 years after the original discovery.

02Drug development isn't speeding up despite AI advances

Unlike Moore's Law, the number of new drugs developed per year is declining, and the drug development pipeline from research through clinical trials can take up to 10 years and cost billions with an acceptance rate around 5% or less. The goal of virtual cell/tissue/human modeling is to compress this timeline, but the speaker notes gains only in early research stages won't help the full pipeline without breakthroughs across every stage.

“the number of drugs that developed each year is kind of declining, which is surprising with all the advances in technology, in AI”

03RNA-seq dominates because it's easiest to scale, but data is noisy

Of the measurement modalities (genome, RNA-seq/transcriptomics, proteomics, morphology), RNA-seq is used most for foundation model training because PCR-based technology makes it easiest to scale, with datasets reaching hundreds of millions to about 1 billion cells. But identical cells don't read the same, measurements are single snapshots of continuous biological processes, and cross-lab/cross-machine measurements are inconsistent, making the data highly heterogeneous and noisy.

04Transformer models underperform relative to their cost

Transformer-based models like SCGPT treat each cell as a sentence and genes as tokens, using BERT-style masked gene-count prediction, but compressing single-cell data into a latent vector loses information. Comprehensive NeurIPS benchmarking on multimodal data and perturbation-response modeling found these expensive-to-train models are not performing as well as comparable models in language and imaging domains, and are sometimes matched or beaten by simple linear models.

“when you, uh, compress this data, we're losing a lot of information. It doesn't preserve the, it doesn't preserve the information”

05Flow matching models perform better

Flow-matching models such as PrimeFlow, which predict the data distribution starting from Gaussian noise rather than compressing and decoding, outperform transformer/autoencoder-based models like CPA in benchmark comparisons, because autoencoder approaches tend to just predict the mean rather than capture the true distribution.

Commentary (ours)

The pattern of transformer scaling underperforming simple baselines in single-cell biology (unlike language/vision) suggests the field may need a distribution-modeling paradigm shift before scale alone helps -- worth watching whether flow-matching approaches get the compute investment that autoregressive transformers currently receive (ours).
