When Will The Benchmaxxing Plague End?

SpeakerNick Heiner · Surge AI Watchyoutu.be/-npY6XjM8CQ
Evals & benchmarksData curation

If you only read one thing

  • Benchmaxing is defined as labs training too hard on benchmarks in ways that deviate from what people actually care about, and contamination is the default outcome for public benchmarks, not an exception -- Surge found clear evidence Opus had memorized much of SWE-bench Verified (c1, c2).
  • A benchmark that can't distinguish models is broken: Haiku and Fable both scored 20% on one enterprise tool-calling task for opposite reasons, one making mistakes and the other picking a valid but unexpected phone-number format (c3).
  • Labs can game leaderboards directly, e.g. an undisclosed test of 27 models on LM Arena or a watermarked model output telling anonymous crowd voters who to pick; fixing this requires domain experts plus product sense up front, verifier-prompt alignment, and treating high saturation as a sign of broken tasks rather than a real ceiling (c4, c5, c6, c7).
01What benchmaxing is and why it's rampant

Heiner opens by defining benchmaxing as labs training too hard on benchmarks in a way that deviates from real-world value, and argues the term's existence shows the industry already senses benchmarks and reality diverge. He frames the talk around why this happens, whether it's intrinsic to benchmarks, and whether we can ever know which models are actually best.

“Benchmaxing, of course, being when labs are training too hard on benchmarks in a way that deviates from what people actually care about”

02Contamination is the default, not the exception

Heiner says a 1,000-task agentic coding benchmark built by professional engineers costs roughly $15 million to make and $5 million a year to keep current as models improve, which pushes teams toward cheaper AI-assisted or low-cost labor that produces weaker data. He also argues contamination happens by default once questions and answers are public online, citing Surge's own investigation showing Opus had verbatim memorized large parts of SWE-bench Verified, a fact not disclosed in Opus 4.8's model card.

“we found very clear evidence that Opus had memorized a lot of Sweetbench”

▶ watch at 05:39
03A benchmark must actually differentiate models

Heiner cites Automation Bench, where hard-coded string-match verifiers for tasks like phone numbers accept only one valid format without telling the model which one. As a result, Haiku and Fable both score 20% on that task, but for opposite reasons: Haiku makes real mistakes, while Fable gets the task right 80% of the time but often picks a differently formatted, equally valid answer. A task that can't separate a mistake-prone model from a mostly-correct one isn't measuring anything useful.

“Haiku scores 20% because it makes a bunch of mistakes and Fable scores 20% because it gets it right 80% of the time but then just happens to pick different formats”

▶ watch at 06:41
04Labs can game leaderboards directly

Beyond flaws in how benchmarks are built, Heiner describes lab-side tactics: hiring a crowdsourced voting army for LM Arena and having the model include a watermark telling anonymous voters which response to pick, and running evals under unrepresentative conditions without disclosing them. He cites a paper showing Meta tested 27 models on LM Arena without disclosing that it was doing so, distorting the results other labs and users see.

“you have your model include a watermark that tells the crowd who to vote for”

05Building a benchmark that resists benchmaxing

Heiner's fix starts with great human experts informing every downstream decision, plus product sense specific to the domain -- for a medical benchmark, doctors alone aren't enough without someone who understands the regulatory and legal constraints on deployment. He warns that when labs call a benchmark 'saturated' at 80%, it's often unclear whether that reflects a real capability ceiling or simply that 20% of the tasks are broken, since you can't tell which until you've solved the rest. Surge's own Hemingway Bench for writing quality is built entirely on blind comparisons from a paid workforce of thousands of professional writers, poets, journalists, and editors, since Heiner argues neither mechanical scoring nor LLM-as-judge has good enough taste for writing.

“It's not enough to have doctors who can answer specific medical questions because if you're trying to test how ready are we for agents to be deployed into hospitals”

Commentary (ours)

Heiner's fixes (expert-authored data, aligned verifiers, holdout sets, human-eval-anchored leaderboards) are consistent across the talk, but they're also expensive at exactly the scale Surge sells into, so the recommendation and Surge's business model point the same direction; that doesn't make the diagnosis wrong, but it's worth weighing the pitch alongside the argument (ours).
