Generative Video at the Speed of Light

SpeakerKeegan McCallum · uRun Watchyoutu.be/Xln-On3syJk
Generative mediaInfra & serving

If you only read one thing

  • uRun's Helios (a distilled Wan 2.1 14B) generates video at about 1/100th the cost of frontier batch models, at comparable quality to last year's frontier. (c1, c2)
  • At current pricing, $10 buys about 3 hours of continuously generated video and $50 buys roughly a full day (15 hours). (c4)
  • At least 40 models with real-time or long-horizon generation capability shipped this year, which McCallum frames as reason to build serving infrastructure now. (c3)
01Quality and efficiency are separate improvement axes

McCallum contrasts the well-known quality trajectory (2023's 'Will Smith eating spaghetti' through Sora, Sora 2, and SeeDance) with a second, less-discussed axis: efficiency and long-horizon generation. Helios, the model uRun serves, is described as a distill of Wan 2.1 14B and produces output at roughly the quality bar of last year's frontier models.

02Cost has dropped roughly 100x for comparable quality

uRun's real-time demo runs at about 1/100th the cost of the equivalent minutes-long batch generation, with arguably better motion. McCallum ties this to a broader trend: at least 40 models with real-time or long-horizon capability were released this year.

“it was generated for about a 100th of the cost”

03What $10-$50 buys today

At current pricing, $10 of generation buys about 3 continuous hours of video and $50 buys close to a full day (roughly 15 hours) of interacting with an AI in a visual medium.

“$10 can get you 3 hours worth of generated video continuously with most of these models and $50 would give you an entire day interacting with an AI in a visual medium”

▶ watch at 03:36
04Use cases beyond games: magic mirrors, accessibility, steerable content

McCallum lists a magic-mirror use case (transforming a live webcam feed into different outfits or settings), accessibility (visual, non-text interaction for people who think visually rather than in text), and content creation, where creators can steer generation live instead of the current slot-machine approach of prompting and spending roughly $10/minute hoping for the right shot.

“we've very much had a slot machine type approach where you're setting up a prompt and maybe some key”

▶ watch at 04:39

“there's more opportunities to have companions or visual mediums that are going to allow more people to experience the things a lot of us have with coding models”

▶ watch at 04:08
05Building this requires a serving layer, not just a model

McCallum argues real-time applications need globally distributed GPUs, WebRTC/ICE/TURN setup, and multi-model streaming pipelines synchronized frame-by-frame with user controls. uRun's answer is a React component for the frontend and a programmable Python runtime behind it, plus a CLI/MCP server so agents can build these pipelines directly. He frames 2026 as needing 'software factories,' not just platforms.

“what if there was just a React component that you could drop into your application”

▶ watch at 06:45

“in 2026, don't just need platforms, we need software factories and ways for agents interact with these”

▶ watch at 07:18

Commentary (ours)

The cost and hours-per-dollar figures are a useful anchor but are vendor-stated and not independently verified against a specific hardware/model config (ours) — worth re-checking against your own workload before using them to size a build-vs-buy decision.
