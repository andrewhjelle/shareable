01Gemma 4 lineup and license

Gemma 4, released a week before the talk, is described as Google's most capable open model family to date, spanning 2 billion to 32 billion parameters with the smallest two models able to run on Android, iOS, and even a Raspberry Pi. With this release Google also switched Gemma's license to a full Apache 2.0 license, a response to prior feedback that the older license wasn't permissive enough.

02Per-layer embeddings: the E in E2B

The smallest models use a 'per-layer embeddings' architecture, denoted by the E in names like E2B and E4B, where a nominally 4-billion-parameter model only needs about 2 billion parameters loaded into GPU memory. The per-layer embedding piece works more like a lookup table than a matrix computation, so it can live in CPU memory or even on disk rather than requiring GPU residency, which is what makes the smallest Gemma models viable on phones.

“you have probably never heard about the E here. So E2B stands for effectively 2 billion parameters”

▶ watch at 05:44

“you don't need to have this in the GPU, you can have this in the CPU, you can have this in the disk”

03On-device parallel throughput

In a live demo, 10 separate Gemma instances ran in parallel on a laptop via llama.cpp, each generating a different SVG at roughly 100 tokens per second, fully offline with no API calls.

“All of these running on device with llama.cpp. And even then it's like 100 tokens per second”

▶ watch at 03:08
04Multilingual reach and ecosystem scale

Gemma 4 was trained on over 140 languages using a tokenizer derived from Gemini's multilingual work, which the speaker says makes fine-tuning for low-resource languages work reasonably well out of the box. Community adoption is presented as large: over 1,000 models have already been built on top of Gemma 4 in the roughly one week since release.

“So Gemma 4 has well, it was trained with over 140 languages and it uses the tokenizer that is based on Gemini as well”

05Beyond chat: applied research

The speaker cites a DeepMind paper from December of the prior year in which researchers used Gemma 3 to propose cancer therapy pathways that were then taken to an actual lab and validated, offered as an example of open models being used for concrete scientific work beyond conversational assistants.
