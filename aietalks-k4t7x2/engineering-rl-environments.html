01RL environments are scaling frontier-lab training

The talk opens by noting that RL environments have become a funded area with startups working directly with major AI labs, citing DeepSeek and MiniMax technical reports that describe using thousands of reinforcement learning environments to improve model performance on hard tasks.

“Recent technical reports by DeepSeek and MiniMax showed that they are effectively using thousands of reinforcement learning environments”

▶ watch at 01:02
02RLVR vs. supervised fine-tuning: exploring instead of imitating

In reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), a model generates a reasoning trace and answer that gets checked against a known-correct answer, with the outcome as the training signal. This is presented as fundamentally different from SFT: SFT completions stay close to the distribution of curated examples, while RLVR lets the model explore trajectories from its own pre-training and learns to favor whichever ones maximize reward.

“The answer is then checked against the known correct answer. The reward is used for reinforcement learning training.”

03Verifiers: environments as installable Python packages

Verifiers, an open-source library from Prime Intellect, provides base classes (single-turn, multi-turn, tool, MCP, stateful-tool environments) so that RL environments can be built, evaluated, and trained as distributable Python packages rather than one-off scripts tied to a specific training stack.

“Environments are Python packages that can be easily installed and distributed.”

▶ watch at 10:28
04Case study: turning a small model into a tic-tac-toe master

Starting from LFM-2 (a small open instruct model from Liquid AI that initially struggled to follow move format), the speaker generated synthetic SFT data with GPT-5 mini, ran a warm-up SFT pass, then trained with GRPO/CISPO reinforcement learning using a custom tic-tac-toe environment with a controllable-skill opponent. The resulting model draws 85% of games against an optimal minimax opponent and rarely makes invalid moves.

“It dominates random players and draws 85% of the time against an optimal opponent.”

▶ watch at 31:30
05Failure modes and practical lessons

Two specific failures are named: training with a batch size below roughly 256 caused reward instability and model collapse because the model was learning from too few games and opponent types per update; and using a minimax implementation with a hidden tie-breaking bias (always picking the first free cell among equally-scored moves) led the model to memorize that one opponent's pattern rather than learn general tic-tac-toe strategy, producing misleadingly good benchmark numbers.

“If this number is low, this means learning to play from a very small number of matches and opponent types at once.”

▶ watch at 30:25

“I got great benchmark results, but then playing against the model, I realized that it was clueless.”
