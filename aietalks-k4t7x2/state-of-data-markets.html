If you only read one thing

  • Jason Wei's Verifier's Law says ease of training a model on a task is proportional to how verifiable that task is; coding matured first because GitHub supplied objective, high-consensus, high-volume verification for free (c1, c2).
  • Most public benchmarks are built by hiring non-expert domain workers to generate and cherry-pick tasks, then the same data is sold to hill-climb that benchmark, which the speaker calls Goodhart's law with a profit motive (c3, c4).
  • Rubric analysis on real finance tasks found Opus 4.8 scored worse than 4.7, and GPT 5.5 and Opus 4.08 landed within three points of each other but via opposite failure modes, arithmetic versus methodology (c5, c6).

Why coding got good first: Verifier's Law

  • The speaker cites researcher Jason Wei's Verifier's Law: how easily a model can be trained on a task tracks how verifiable that task is, broken into three axes (asymmetry, veracity, and proliferation of verification).
  • Coding scored high on all three because GitHub already supplied unit tests as objective correctness checks, broad community consensus on what working code means, and effectively unlimited public examples with commit messages as free reasoning traces.

“The ease of training a model to do a task is proportional to how verifiable the task is.”

said at 06:13

“The community agrees on what working code means generally. And there are effectively infinite public examples with these commit messages as basically free reasoning traces.”

02Why most benchmarks are quietly fake
Slide at 07:50 · click any slide to enlarge · 1 more from this section below

Why most benchmarks are quietly fake

  • The dominant recipe for building a benchmark is to hire domain experts, have them use chat tools to generate plausible tasks, solve those tasks, cherry-pick the cases where the model diverged, and package the result as a hard benchmark, then sell similar data back to labs to hill-climb that same benchmark.
  • The speaker argues a single benchmark score under a single scaffold is one noisy sample from a distribution nobody has actually measured.

“let's have them chat use chat to generate plausible tasks. Let's have them solve those tasks, and let's cherry-pick the ones where the model diverged, and let's package them as a hard North Star benchmark.”

said at 08:18

“a single benchmark number under a single scaffold is like basically one sample from a distribution who's basically with nobody measured”

03Recent frontier models trade off differently under rubric scoring

Recent frontier models trade off differently under rubric scoring

  • Running real-world, long-horizon finance tasks (an ARR waterfall reconciliation, an LBO valuation memo, a long-short pair trade) through deterministic verifiers plus LLM-as-judge, the speaker found Opus 4.8 scored worse than 4.7 on several rubrics, which he attributes to over-engineered self-reflection introduced in post-training.
  • GPT 5.5 and Opus 4.08 landed within three points of each other overall but failed in opposite ways, GPT nailing arithmetic while losing methodology and Opus the reverse.

“Opus 4.8 is worse than 4.7 on a lot of these rubrics.”

said at 10:22

“GPT 5.5 and Opus 4.08 score within three points on the same task. They fell in like exactly opposite directions, whereas GPT nails the arithmetic”

said at 10:53
04Lab data-vendor spend as a leading indicator

Lab data-vendor spend as a leading indicator

  • The speaker proposes tracking which data categories labs are buying as a predictor of upcoming products: Anthropic's vendor spend shifted toward cybersecurity data in January and biological data in March/April, and specific products (named as Metis, Cybor, and Claude bio/life sciences offerings) followed 2-3 months later.

“Anthropic was spending a lot on cybersecurity data from new vendors. And in March and April, they were spending a lot on biological data”

05Where the durable value sits
Slide at 17:20 · click any slide to enlarge · 1 more from this section below

Where the durable value sits

  • The speaker's closing argument is that data companies are converging into 'neo labs' serving enterprise, and that the defensible asset for a builder is not the dataset itself but the pipeline into real, ongoing work plus the infrastructure to keep retraining on it as underlying models change.

“your moat's not the data, it's the sort of pipeline into real-world work. Plus the infra to keep retraining on it as the models improve underneath you.”

said at 17:07

Commentary (ours)
