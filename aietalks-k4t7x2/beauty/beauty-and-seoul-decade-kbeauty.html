01Starting Beauty & Seoul in 2016

Maree said that when she founded Beauty & Seoul in 2016, investors and major UK/US online beauty retailers she pitched turned down the idea of curating Korean skincare, telling her they didn't think the category would become popular.

“we spoke to investors at the time and a lot of the big online beauty retailers that you all, you know, know and love and they said, oh yeah, we're not really interested in curating Korean skin care.”

02COVID as the turning point

She said the shift from niche to mainstream took roughly three to four years and points to COVID as the inflection point, as people turned to self-care and online shopping during lockdowns. She also said today's shoppers are more ingredient-literate than in the brand's early years, reading up on formulations rather than just following trends.

“I think it's really interesting because like I said, it was a really hard sell, I would say, probably for the first three, four years. I think the changing point was COVID.”

“the consumer is more savvy than ever now, especially with skin care. You know, they read up about ingredients.”

03What actually differentiates Korean formulations

Maree attributes Korean skincare's reputation to intense domestic competition: she describes hundreds of new Korean brands launching constantly, pushing companies to compete on formulation and ingredient quality, citing sunscreen formulation as an example. She said that head-to-head, Korean products are often comparable in quality to Western brands at the same ingredient percentage, but significantly cheaper.

“a lot, you know, a lot of the Koreans SPFs, they have really elegant formulations, they absorb quickly, that's a must, they have to be non-greasy because summers in Korea are so stifling and humid.”

“a lot of products with Western brands, they're very similar in terms of quality, but they will be significantly cheaper on cost for the same level of ingredient percentage, for example.”

04A safety concern, and a growing tourism channel

She raised concern over retailers marketing actives to younger customers, citing reports that Sephora had to scale back sending certain PR packages to preteens, and said children don't need retinol at age 12. Separately, she described K-beauty tourism as extending beyond shopping and cosmetic procedures to treatments like Rejuran and laser procedures, which she said are significantly cheaper in South Korea than comparable options abroad.

“I think Sephora had to increase that cuz they were sending out PR packages to essentially, you know, preteens. And you know, you don't need to use a retinol when you're 12.”
