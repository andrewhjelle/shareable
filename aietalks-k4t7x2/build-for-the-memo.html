If you only read one thing

  • Chan frames two different bars: a demo only needs to sound plausible for five minutes, while a memo has to survive an adversarial committee reviewing sources that disagree; he argues investment decisions run on trust, not intelligence (c1, c2).
  • He details concrete trust-breaking failures: an unweighted retrieval system, an unreconciled 18% vs 17.4% internal inconsistency, a real-estate pricing algorithm write-off, a lawyer's brief citing six fabricated court cases the chatbot then vouched for as real, and an airline chatbot inventing a bereavement-fare policy that a tribunal held the airline liable for (c4, c5, c6, c7, c8).
  • His proposed fix is five requirements: every claim links to a sourced receipt, facts and guesses stay visually separate, numbers auto-reconcile, contradictions get surfaced rather than smoothed over, and every decision ends with a logged, accountable human signer (c9, c10).

01Demo vs memo: two different bars

Demo vs memo: two different bars

  • Chan, a 15-year investment banker turned investor, defines a demo as a system whose only job is to make a room say "oh" for five minutes, versus a memo, which has to survive a real committee working through hundreds of pages of disagreeing sources.
  • He argues that in his experience across roughly 200 investment committee meetings, deals get approved on trust rather than on the intelligence of the analysis.

“Money doesn't follow intelligence. Money follows trust, and the trust is fragile, especially when the thing that wrote the page has never once in its entire life said the words, "I'm not sure."”

said at 04:13

“Machine one is a demo. One clean document in, one fluent answer out is the whole job is to make a room go oh for 5 minutes.”

02When a demo fails the memo test anyway
Slide at 07:10 · click any slide to enlarge · 2 more from this section below

When a demo fails the memo test anyway

  • Chan cites the February 2023 case of a major tech company's AI assistant demo giving a wrong answer about a space telescope, after which the company's stock dropped roughly 8% in a day, wiping out about $100 billion in value from a single unchecked sentence.
  • His point is that once real money is watching, every demo sentence is effectively already a memo sentence.

“the market noticed the company's stock dropped around 8% in a day. That's roughly $100 billion of value gone because of unchecked sentence.”

03Six ways trust breaks

Six ways trust breaks

  • Chan walks through six recurring product failures: retrieval systems that treat an audited filing and an overheard rumor as equally trustworthy; internal numbers that don't reconcile (an 18% headline growth figure against a 17.4% table elsewhere); a real-estate pricing algorithm that ran unsupervised and led to a roughly half-billion-dollar write-off and unit shutdown; a lawyer's court filing citing six fabricated cases that the same chatbot then confirmed were real when asked; and an airline chatbot that invented a bereavement-fare policy, leading a tribunal to hold the airline liable rather than accept that the chatbot was a separate responsible entity.

04The fix: five product requirements
Slide at 20:40 · click any slide to enlarge · 2 more from this section below

The fix: five product requirements

  • Chan's prescription, framed as what he demands from a vendor before using their system near a live deal: every claim carries a sourced receipt to its origin paragraph and trust level, facts and guesses are visually distinguished, numbers reconcile automatically without manual checking, contradictions between sources get surfaced rather than smoothed into a single answer, and every decision ends with a logged, accountable human approver.
  • He argues an architecture without a fundable human at the end is not a product but an excuse generator.

“If your architecture doesn't have a fundable human at the end of it, you you have not built a product. You have have built an excuse generator.”

said at 20:28

“every claim comes with a receipt. Each sentence linked us straight to its source paragraph with the source trust level attached.”

said at 21:00

Commentary (ours)

(ours) The five fixes read as a fairly direct checklist against the six failure stories, which makes the talk unusually testable as a product rubric — a team could audit an existing AI product against each of the five requirements directly rather than needing to interpret vaguer trust language.
