The Great Loops Debate

SpeakerDex Horthy, Geoff Huntley, Ian Livingston, Greg Pstrucha (moderated by Ali Howard) · Human Layer / Key Card / Sentry (panel) Watchyoutu.be/c35YoMdnI78
Agentic engineeringCoding agentsContext engineering

If you only read one thing

  • Pro-loop panelists Geoff Huntley and Ian Livingston argue loops are an inevitable, already-in-use extension of software engineering's core try/learn/apply cycle (c1, c2)
  • Skeptic panelists Dex Horthy and Greg Pstrucha counter that hype is outrunning discipline and that the token economics of stacking loops on loops aren't sustainable at scale (c3, c6)

01How the debate was structured

Moderator Ali Howard framed the session as an Oxford-style debate between two teams: Ian Livingston and Geoff Huntley arguing there is no delta between the hype around loops and what works in practice, versus Dex Horthy and Greg Pstrucha arguing there is a real delta. Each panelist opened with a timed monologue, and the winner was to be judged by how many audience members changed their stance by the end. Geoff Huntley opened for the pro-loop side by describing loops as an inevitable, if imperfect, new way of working.

“loops are uh somewhat uh whilst Ralph might be a bit of a meme and cetera, there was actually deep thought into it”

02Why loops feel inevitable to their proponents

Geoff Huntley, creator of the Ralph loop, described applying loop-based agent orchestration as treating the process like a new CPU architecture, arguing there was more deliberate thought behind it than its meme reputation suggests. Ian Livingston separately argued that loops aren't new at all -- that a try/learn/apply loop has always been at the core of software development, and that current tooling is simply compressing that same loop and shifting judgment away from humans.

“loops are uh somewhat uh whilst Ralph might be a bit of a meme and cetera, there was actually deep thought into it”

“it is a is a loop is at the core of I try something, I learn something, I apply something”

03Where the skeptics push back

Dex Horthy argued the core problem isn't whether loops work but that hype is outrunning the discipline needed to use them well, framing the industry's search for a loop-based silver bullet as an attempt to avoid the hard part of the job (code review). Greg Pstrucha raised a separate economic objection: stacking loops on loops to paper over quality problems has real, and in his view unsustainable, token-spend costs at company scale.

“the hype is is making us feel like there's a magic answer to this and it requires a lot more thought and care than the uh the the Twitter sphere would have you believe”

▶ watch at 09:50

“that brings me to my second point, which is the economic viability of of the way that we are using agents today. And I don't believe that this is sustainable”

▶ watch at 14:29
04Security, alignment, and accountability gaps

Asked directly about agent alignment, Ian Livingston said he has no confidence that models can be kept aligned or judged safe as they scale, describing them as increasingly goal-seeking and skilled at finding exploits. Later in the debate he separately said that access-control and commit-attribution systems (e.g., git allowing only one signer per commit) were not designed for a world where agents act on a human's behalf, calling it an unsolved problem. Jeff Huntley's practical counter to bad agent behavior is heavy use of pre-commit hooks as an engineered feedback backstop.

“I'm totally not convinced that that's possible. In fact, I think what we've seen is as we scale with these models and as we use reinforcement learning, they're inherently incredibly goal-seeking”

“This is an unsolved problem, first and foremost. Like, let's be really honest that our our access control systems weren't designed for this this world”

“I heavily exploit pre-commit hooks, folks. Um and I engineer in that back pressure uh by analyzing the work that is done”

05Where the panel actually converges

Despite team assignments, Dex Horthy said the realistic, sustainable gain from coding agents today is a two-to-three-times speedup, and cautioned against chasing a 100x speedup at the cost of losing the architectural ownership engineers already have. Geoff Huntley, arguing for the pro-loop side, still predicted that within a year the same conference would be full of talks about software factories and loops failing.

“if every software engineer in the world was two to three x faster and had a like near human 99% level human level of quality, that would change every single enterprise in the world”

▶ watch at 49:04

“I have my deepest concerns next this time next year at the conference. We're going to see a whole bunch of talks saying how factories fail and how loops fail”

Commentary (ours)

The framing as a two-sided debate mostly breaks down in the actual exchange: panelists on opposite "teams" agreed on more than they disagreed on (all four favor some verification-gated loop use, none claim full autonomy works today), and the real fault line running through the talk is less pro/anti-loop than how much token spend and unresolved infrastructure (access control, commit attribution, model alignment) they're each willing to tolerate before calling something production-ready.
