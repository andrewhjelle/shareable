04Month by month — in their words

Narrative (ours, hedged) with the evidence it leans on, then three talks per month chosen mechanically: the most-viewed talks whose strongest claim is a prediction or a warning. Quotes are verbatim and time-linked.

The arc (ours)

Three months of AI Engineer talks, published on YouTube in batches, show the channel's editorial cadence more than when ideas emerged. Only 217 of 423 briefed talks carry an upload date because YouTube blocked date lookups, so the timeline covers the dated subset: 6 talks in June, 143 in July, 68 in August through the 21st. June's tiny sample and August's partial window make both indicative rather than definitive. Across the months, attention shifts from wiring individual agents reliably, to a crowded debate over harnesses, evals, and the forward-deployed engineer, to a late-summer pivot toward continual learning, computer-use browsers, and real-time video. Reads to us (ours) as the community absorbing agent-building as table stakes and moving toward what keeps them improving.

Aug 2026

71 talks · 588 claims · top themes: Agentic engineering (37), Evals & benchmarks (19), Infra & serving (17)

August: continual learning, computer use, and real-time video take over

August's 68 dated talks through the 21st push in a different direction. Continual learning becomes the loudest single topic, especially on the 12th. Yu Su of NeoCognition argues a strong continual-learning algorithm could deliver unbounded expertise from bounded intelligence, Ronak Malde of Trajectory calls software that gets smarter every use the most exciting unlock for 2026-2027, and Sara Hooker of Adaption says pre-training size is no longer the most lucrative axis of scale. Second, computer-use browsers get their own room. Dhruv Batra of Yutori argues computer-use agents will "identify the web not APIs," and Paul Klein of Browserbase says solving computer use accelerates the diffusion of AI into the real economy. Third, real-time generative video shows up as a category on the 18th, with Sidney Primas of LemonSlice forecasting end-to-end EQ avatar models within two-to-three years. Meanwhile Itamar Friedman of Qodo asks whether human PR review will still be mandatory by end of 2026. Reads to us (ours) as the substrate for durable improvement, not the harness debate, becoming the front-line question.

Jul 2026

172 talks · 1427 claims · top themes: Agentic engineering (116), Evals & benchmarks (56), Coding agents (42)

July: agents, harnesses, and the forward-deployed engineer flood the stage

July is the flood, 143 talks and 1,185 claims. Two conversations dominate. First, harnesses. Sam Bhagwat of Mastra names Steinberger's law: every harness expands until it becomes a claw, with only a limited number surviving per category. The Great Loops Debate on July 17 openly split, with Geoff Huntley of Human Layer predicting a wave of talks by next year on how factories and loops have failed, while Dex Horthy pegs the sustainable speedup at two-to-three times, not 100x. Second, the forward-deployed engineer arrives as a category. Pauline Brunet of Cursor calls FDE the 2026 hottest job, Kevin Bai of Anthropic ties its rise to platforms turning agentic, and Natalie Meurer of Sierra closes with "forward deployed engineering is dead. And long live forward deployed engineering." Underneath, evals get treated as durable moat by Brendan Rappazzo of Morgan Stanley, and Barr Yaron reports 67 percent of respondents expect a lab to declare AGI within five years. Reads to us (ours) as the field getting crowded with sharp disagreement.

Jun 2026

72 talks · 605 claims · top themes: Agentic engineering (43), Infra & serving (25), Evals & benchmarks (21)

June: a small sample worries about reliability, cost, and context load

June's dated set is only six talks so patterns are indicative, not conclusive. The through-line the speakers themselves emphasize is that agent reliability now lives in the plumbing. Nishant Gupta of Meta Superintelligence Labs argues "The next frontier is infrastructure," and warns autonomous agents violate the deterministic assumptions cloud infrastructure was designed around, so a bad retry loop can turn a minor API error into a compute incident. Matt Pocock of AI Hero and Justin Schroeder of Standard Agents both name context load as the tax on adding skills. Rajkumar Sakthivel of Tesco puts numbers on it, reporting that a typical query on his project sent 45,000 tokens of context when only 5,000 mattered, and that 90 percent of AI coding cost is input, not output. Balázs Horváth of VisualLabs pushes back on demo-as-deliverable. Reads to us (ours) as the community discovering that the hard problem is running the agent, not writing it.
