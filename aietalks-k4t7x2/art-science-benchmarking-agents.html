The Art & Science of Benchmarking Agents

SpeakerVincent Chen · Snorkel AI Watchyoutu.be/iNkFlCiij0U
Evals & benchmarksAgentic engineeringData curation

If you only read one thing

  • Snorkel committed $3M to an Open Benchmarks Grant and had reviewed over 120 applications at the time of the talk (c1, c2).
  • Good benchmarks combine rigorous task validation, distributional diversity, unsaturated difficulty, and evaluation beyond raw accuracy — GPQA's multi-reviewer protocol, MMLU's 57-domain taxonomy, and ARC-AGI-3 launching with frontier models under 1% are cited as examples (c3, c4, c5).

02Task quality and distribution: the 'science' side

The speaker holds up GPQA's adversarial quality-control process as a model for individual task rigor: a multi-reviewer protocol with an original author, reviewers, and adjudicators, with incentive payouts tied to reviewer agreement. MMLU is cited for distributional diversity, having built a taxonomy of 57 academic and professional domains across STEM and humanities that it distributed tasks against intentionally rather than haphazardly.

“they had a very very rigorous multi-reviewer protocol where there was an original author, you know, there were there were reviewers and adjudicators in the loop”

“constructed a a quite ambitious taxonomy of, you know, 57 academic and and professional domains across STEM, humanities, et cetera”

03Difficulty, headroom, and evaluation beyond accuracy

Benchmarks need enough model headroom to keep separating frontier systems; ARC-AGI-3 launched with frontier models scoring under 1% on human-solvable tasks. Tau-Bench is cited for going beyond task-completion accuracy to check policy adherence, using the example of an agent that books the correct flight but violates fare-class rules and should still be scored as a failure.

“they had frontier models under 1%. Every single task was a human solvable to some degree”

▶ watch at 10:55

“a model that books the right flight, but violates fair class rules, still fails, is still a kind of um no-go at the end of the day”

▶ watch at 12:28
04Thesis, roadmap, and researcher UX: the 'art' side

The most influential benchmarks stake out a thesis about where the field is going: Terminal-Bench bet on the CLI as a general-purpose computer-use interface, not just for coding agents. SWE-bench is cited for spawning an entire benchmark family (Lite, Verified, Pro, multilingual, multimodal) and reshaping how the field thinks about coding agents. The speaker also argues that researcher/builder UX (ease of running, extending, and contributing to a benchmark) is an underrated adoption driver.

“It was a bet on the CLI not just for coding agents, but for general purpose computer use”

“the most prescient benchmark builders are committed to the researcher and builder experience”

05Where Snorkel thinks the next benchmarks need to go

The speaker proposes three axes for future benchmarks: environment complexity (realistic, dynamic operating conditions like org-specific policies and flaky toolchains), autonomy horizon (how long an agent can operate before reliability breaks down), and output complexity (richer artifacts and reward signals beyond chat or document answers, including agents expressing uncertainty).

“How complex, how realistic, how dynamic is the operating environment that these benchmarks are working?”

▶ watch at 18:43

Commentary (ours)

The pairing of GPQA-style adversarial review with a $3M open grant suggests Snorkel is trying to institutionalize benchmark rigor the way peer review does for research, which is worth watching if it produces benchmarks with staying power comparable to SWE-bench or MMLU rather than one-off leaderboard entries (ours).
