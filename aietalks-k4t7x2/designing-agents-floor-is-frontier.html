takeEvals & benchmarksAgentic engineering

Argues that eval-set investment is the wrong lever once an agent ships, because harnesses and models change too fast for a fixed eval suite to survive

If you only read one thing

  • Ceiling (best-case capability) matters less to user trust than floor (worst-case failure); floor-raising is the practical work.
  • Clustering traces for issue detection doesn't scale — it can't reliably track when an issue started or how fast it's growing.
  • Agents are bad at finding anomalies themselves; feed them deterministic signals (like keyword-frequency spikes) and have them investigate, not detect.

01Evals as fixed test sets don't survive a changing stack
Slide at 06:10 · click any slide to enlarge · 1 more from this section below

Evals as fixed test sets don't survive a changing stack

  • Hylak argues most public discourse on evals is stuck in the earlier chatbot era, where a fixed eval set made sense because the space of possible answers was narrow.
  • With agents, the harness and model change constantly, so eval sets built around a specific tool-calling pattern break the moment you switch harnesses (he cites an 80% eval failure rate after a switch to Claude Code CLI).
  • His recommendation is to treat evals as code, unit and end-to-end tests run locally, rather than as a slow-changing curated set or managed prompt playground.

02Floor over ceiling
Slide at 11:20 · click any slide to enlarge · 1 more from this section below

Floor over ceiling

  • He frames agent quality as a ceiling (best emergent capability) versus a floor (worst thing the agent can do, recommend a competitor, delete data, send an unwanted email).
  • User trust is broken by floor incidents, not by capability gaps, so floor-raising is where practical effort should go.

“the floor is like what is the worst thing your agent can do”

said at 10:16
03Two facts every issue needs
Slide at 13:00 · click any slide to enlarge · 2 more from this section below

Two facts every issue needs

  • For any discovered issue, Raindrop's practice is to always establish two things: when it started, and what percentage of users it affects.
  • Knowing an issue is new (versus long-standing) reframes it as something caused by a recent change; knowing the affected percentage sets the urgency.

“you need to know when it actually started, and you need to know how many people it affects”

said at 13:20
04Clustering traces is a one-off tool, not a monitoring system
Slide at 15:20 · click any slide to enlarge

Clustering traces is a one-off tool, not a monitoring system

  • A common naive approach is to cluster all traces to find issues.
  • Hylak says this is useful for one-off analysis but doesn't scale as ongoing monitoring, because clusters can't reliably track when something started or how it's growing over time, and cluster boundaries (what counts as "the same issue") are inconsistent across products.

“the first one is that clusters are not issues”

said at 15:54

“it's useful for one-off analysis, but it just doesn't really scale well”

05Don't ask agents to detect anomalies — ask them to investigate ones you've found
Slide at 18:30 · click any slide to enlarge · 2 more from this section below

Don't ask agents to detect anomalies — ask them to investigate ones you've found

  • His closing lesson: agents are poor at anomaly detection on their own.
  • The workable pattern is to pull out deterministic signals (e.g., keyword-frequency spikes) first, then hand the agent a specific anomaly to investigate rather than asking it to scan for problems unprompted.

“agents are very, very bad at anomaly detection. So don't ask your agent to find anomalies”

said at 18:27

“ask it to investigate anomalies you've already found”

said at 18:27

Commentary (ours)

The floor/ceiling framing is a useful lens for prioritizing agent QA work, though it's worth noting Raindrop sells exactly this kind of production monitoring, so the pitch against clustering and fixed eval sets doubles as a product argument (ours).
