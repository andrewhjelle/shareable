01Scale and the trust problem

Scale and the trust problem

  • Uber Eats operates at roughly a $90 billion annual run rate across 10,000 cities, and photos are often the first signal a customer sees about a merchant, but smaller merchants often lack quality photography.
  • The team frames this as a trust problem: a meaningful fraction of consumers distrust anything that looks AI-generated, so the system has to enhance selectively without making the marketplace look artificial or homogeneous.

02Where agents fit on the control spectrum

Where agents fit on the control spectrum

  • The team frames agent design as a spectrum between deterministic, rules-based systems (controllable but brittle) and giving an agent creativity and agency (scalable but requiring guardrails).
  • They deliberately lean toward agency while keeping it constrained by safety requirements.

“that's actually what we want to lean into, but we can't leave that unconstrained, right?”

03Start with logging
Slide at 03:30 · click any slide to enlarge

Start with logging

  • The end-to-end pipeline -- image understanding/routing, image editing with a self-correcting QA loop, and final post-processing QA -- logs every agent's output into a single flat JSON structure so both technical and non-technical staff can diagnose individual cases and roll up aggregates.
  • The team treats this logging as the prerequisite for having anything to optimize or for setting up a self-learning loop at all.

“you want to start with your logging cuz if you don't start with it, you have nothing to optimize for, let alone set up a self-learning loop”

said at 06:42
04Guardrail metric: recall, and what happens when it misses
Slide at 10:00 · click any slide to enlarge · 1 more from this section below

Guardrail metric: recall, and what happens when it misses

  • For the routing agent, the chosen guardrail metric is recall, since letting a bad image through is worse than over-flagging a good one.
  • Concrete failure examples included the router flagging an already-high-quality cheeseburger photo as low-quality (wasting compute and risking degradation) and approving an image showing six chicken wings against a listing that said eight, risking the enhancement model hallucinating the missing pieces.

“For routing, our guardrail metric is recall. We don't want any bad image to slip through our system.”

said at 09:18

“there's a chance your model's going to hallucinate these two extra wings to to match the description”

05Closed-loop auto-tuning and reward hacking in enhancement
Slide at 10:50 · click any slide to enlarge · 8 more from this section below

Closed-loop auto-tuning and reward hacking in enhancement

  • Production data is periodically sampled, sent to human labelers, and compared against agent output; mismatches go to a diagnoser agent that localizes the issue and triggers an auto-tuning pipeline with no human in the loop, re-benchmarking against the golden set before shipping a new config.
  • The image-enhancement step measures pass-at-K (pass rate by iteration count) but showed reward hacking, where an agent that got rejected for an overly creative edit oversteered into a generic, overly conservative fallback plate instead of a meaningfully better one.

“this is completely config driven and doesn't require human in the loop. Your diagnoser agent can write your config and trigger the auto-tuning pipeline”

said at 11:56

“Pass at K is essentially the pass rate at Kth iteration.”

said at 14:30

“it actually oversteers the other other way. And it becomes overly conservative.”

said at 16:03

Commentary (ours)

(ours) The recall-first guardrail choice for routing is the clearest transferable decision in the talk: it explicitly accepts wasted enhancement compute on already-good images as the cost of avoiding a worse failure mode (bad images reaching the menu), which is a trade-off worth stating explicitly whenever a team is picking a single guardrail metric.
