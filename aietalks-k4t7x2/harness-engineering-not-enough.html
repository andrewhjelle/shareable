Harness Engineering is not Enough: Why Software Factories Fail

patternCoding agentsEvals & benchmarksAgentic engineering

Horthy argues that 'lights-off' fully autonomous coding pipelines fail for reasons that can't be engineered around with better prompts or more tokens, because current RL training rewards passing tests, not maintainable architecture

If you only read one thing

  • Real-world data (Faros AI) shows PR review quality and merge-without-review rates worsening since teams adopted coding agents broadly.
  • This is a model-training limitation, not a skill issue: RL rewards tests passing, with no way to penalize eroded maintainability.
  • Fix is re-adding upfront product/architecture/program-design review; ~30 minutes of planning can save hours of PR review time.

The 'lights-off' factory is producing worse code

02This is a training problem, not a prompting problem
Slide at 02:30 · click any slide to enlarge · 8 more from this section below

This is a training problem, not a prompting problem

  • He argues no amount of harness engineering or looped prompting can fix what is fundamentally a model-training issue: RL on coding benchmarks like SWE-bench rewards a binary pass/fail on tests, with no mechanism to penalize architectural erosion or shotgun-surgery-style coupling.
  • Because bad-architecture costs surface months later, the reward signal can't propagate back to the training episode that caused it.

“this is in fact not a skill issue. That no amount of harness engineering or loops maxing can solve what is fundamentally a model training issue”

said at 02:34

“models have a shortcoming. Um they can't maintain and improve codebase quality over time, not without a a decent amount of human steering”

03Why Claude Code broke out first
Slide at 09:50 · click any slide to enlarge · 8 more from this section below

Why Claude Code broke out first

  • Horthy attributes Claude Code's early lead to being the first case of a model lab training a model directly against the harness it would be distributed in, rather than a third party building a harness on top of someone else's model weights.
  • He cites Claude Code's revenue growth (from nothing to roughly 4, then 9 billion in under a year) as evidence of how much that training-harness alignment mattered.

“this was the first time that a model lab trained a model against the harness that they were going to distribute it to users in”

said at 10:18

“Why did Claude Code go from nothing to 4 billion and I think now they're at 9 billion in revenue in under a year”

04The fix: put planning and review back in front
Slide at 16:30 · click any slide to enlarge · 7 more from this section below

The fix: put planning and review back in front

  • His recommended workaround is re-adding upfront product review, system architecture, and program design (types, method signatures, call stacks) before code generation, so review time shrinks because alignment already happened.
  • He estimates roughly 30 minutes of pre-planning can save hours of PR review, and closes by telling engineers to treat model limitations as a known constraint to design around rather than a reason to stop reading code.

“The main idea here is 30 minutes over here in pre-planning and alignment can save you hours in review”

said at 16:27

“we're engineers, and these are just constraints, and models are good at certain things, and they're not good at other things. And so go figure out how to solve problems given a set of constraints”
