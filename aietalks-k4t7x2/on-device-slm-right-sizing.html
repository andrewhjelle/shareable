If you only read one thing

  • Right-sizing framework: prove feasibility with the largest model, define success criteria and a golden dataset, test small-to-large models against it, then close any accuracy gap with prompt engineering before shipping a small/local model.
  • For a thread-summarization feature, Llama 3.2 3B beat Qwen 2.5 1.5B and Gemma 4 E2B on accuracy/latency trade-off, and a few-shot prompt variant closed the gap to Claude Sonnet (91.7% structural validity, 92.9% factual consistency) while cutting per-day inference cost from about $1 to $0.
  • Small language models (vision, audio, chat/translation) consume roughly a quarter of the energy an LLM uses for the same task, and task-specific models use about half of that again, per the talk's energy-cost bar chart.

01The hidden costs of always reaching for a frontier model

The hidden costs of always reaching for a frontier model

  • The speaker lists costs of one-size-fits-all inference: security (data sent to remote servers risks exposure/leakage), latency (research puts 4 seconds as the limit of user believability, and many large-model calls exceed that), unpredictable third-party cost as agentic workloads multiply token usage, and offline unavailability.
  • She notes token prices have been falling but total inference spend has been rising because agentic workloads consume tokens faster than prices drop.

“found that 4 seconds is the limit of believability for users, and many calls that you will make to large models are going to take longer than 4 seconds”

Small/task-specific models use a fraction of the energy

  • She presents a bar chart estimate where a small language model (SLM) uses about 25% of the energy an LLM uses for an equivalent task, and a task-specific model (e.g., a vision or audio model) uses about half of that again.
  • SLMs range from millions to billions of parameters versus billions to trillions for LLMs, and a 1-billion-parameter model fits in about 2GB at FP16.

03The right-sizing framework: prototype big, deploy small
Slide at 09:00 · click any slide to enlarge · 7 more from this section below

The right-sizing framework: prototype big, deploy small

  • Her four-step process: first prove the task is possible at all using the largest/most capable model; second, define success criteria and collect a golden dataset of human-labeled input-output pairs; third, test models from small to large against that dataset using an eval tool (she used Arize's open-source Phoenix); fourth, select the 'SAGE' model -- the smallest model giving acceptable responses.

“I like to think of this as prototype big, deploy small. Just repeat this to yourself. Prototype big. Think big. Go big. Deploy small”

said at 08:52
04Case study: summarizing social threads for her side project Mima
Slide at 14:00 · click any slide to enlarge · 8 more from this section below

Case study: summarizing social threads for her side project Mima

  • She built a golden dataset of 14 threads (28 examples with two summary formats) and benchmarked Claude Sonnet as baseline against Qwen 2.5 1.5B, Qwen 3 1.7B, Llama 3.2 3B, and Gemma 4 E2B on JSON validity, structural validity, factual consistency, and P50/P95 latency.
  • Llama 3.2 won on the accuracy/latency trade-off at around 90% accuracy, while Qwen 2.5 was fastest but least accurate and Gemma 4 was slowest at around 8 seconds.

“It's got an average latency of 2.9 seconds. It costs about $0.22 $0.22 to run this to run 14 of these tasks”

said at 14:05

“when we take accuracy into account, the winner was actually Llama 3.2, which is the big green circle that's weighing in right here around the 90% for accuracy”

said at 17:09
05Closing the accuracy gap with prompt engineering and post-processing
Slide at 24:20 · click any slide to enlarge · 8 more from this section below

Closing the accuracy gap with prompt engineering and post-processing

  • She tested five prompt variants (baseline, numbered-input, few-shot, strict-rules, chain-of-thought) against Llama 3.2 and found few-shot the best performer, raising structural validity and factual consistency while adding only 200ms latency; strict negative-constraint prompts made results worse.
  • Adding post-processing (truncating over-length output, checking reference counts) plus the few-shot prompt closed the gap to 100% JSON/structural validity, and she attributed the remaining factual-consistency gap partly to Claude being an overly strict LLM judge favoring its own family's output.

“it was the few shot prompt that one that made the biggest improvement”

said at 24:22

“we've got uh 100% JSON validity, structural validity was 100%, factual consistency, there's only a little bit of a disagreement there”

said at 27:29
