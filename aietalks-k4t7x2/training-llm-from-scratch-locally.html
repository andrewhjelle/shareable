02Choosing a tokenizer: character-level trade-offs

Choosing a tokenizer: character-level trade-offs

  • The workshop uses a character-level tokenizer with only 65 tokens (one per distinct character in the training data), chosen because it keeps the bigram space small enough (65x65) to converge fast on a tiny dataset, unlike a full byte-pair-encoding vocabulary which the speaker says would never converge for a model this small.

“in the in our case for on our uh data set is going to be only 65 embeddings essentially cuz there's the 65 different characters that will appear in the in our training data.”

said at 09:51
03Model size and training dynamics on a laptop

Model size and training dynamics on a laptop

  • The trained model has 6 layers, 6 attention heads, a 384 embedding dimension, and a 256-token context window, totaling about 1.8 million parameters -- described as easy to train on most devices.
  • Trained on roughly 1 million tokens/characters of Shakespeare text, training took about 15 minutes on a free Google Colab T4 GPU.
  • Loss starts around 4.17 (the natural log of 65, i.e. random guessing) and the speaker's rule of thumb is that legible words appear around loss 1.5-2.0 and decent output around 1.0-1.2, with values below that risking overfitting on this dataset.

04Where the real quality gains come from: post-training data

Where the real quality gains come from: post-training data

  • Asked how base and reasoning models differ, the speaker says the base architecture is largely shared and the difference comes from post-training data quality.

Commentary (ours)

The workshop's headline number worth remembering is the model-size math: a full byte-pair vocabulary (50k tokens) would have made the embedding table alone bigger than the whole 1.8M-parameter model, which is a concrete illustration of why tokenizer choice is treated as a first-order architecture decision rather than an afterthought (ours).
