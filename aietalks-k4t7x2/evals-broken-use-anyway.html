Evals Are Broken, Use Them Anyway

SpeakerAra Khan · Cline Watchyoutu.be/QuuIywMG4s8
Evals & benchmarksCoding agentsAgentic engineering

If you only read one thing

  • Most people misread evals either as objective truth or as worthless vibes; the fix is heuristics: don't trust a lab's own numbers, wait a few weeks before switching models, and prefer new/precise evals over stale standardized ones (c1, c2, c3, c4).
  • Cline built its own evals from real, opted-in user coding sessions and ran them through Terminal Bench (89 tasks) on Harbor infrastructure because single-turn evals can't capture multi-step agent behavior (c5, c6, c7).
  • Improving a harness score moves through three zones -- obvious bug fixes, nuanced per-model prompt tuning, and overfitting -- and the goal is a good score plus a good vibe check, not just the number (c8, c9).
01Two ways people get evals wrong

The speaker frames most people's misunderstanding of evals as splitting into two camps: an 'objective metrics' camp that treats leaderboard scores as literal truth (e.g. assuming two models with similar scores perform identically), and a 'taste' camp that dismisses evals entirely in favor of vibes. The talk's stance is that evals are neither the end-all-be-all nor useless.

“there's basically two camps of people who are wrong about things, right? So there's two camps of wrong on evals.”

02Three heuristics for using other people's evals

First, don't take a model lab's self-reported eval numbers at face value -- treat them as approximations. Second, stay current but don't be the earliest adopter; let a new model's reputation settle for a couple weeks before switching. Third, prefer new and precise evals over old standardized ones, citing OpenAI's own statement that SWE-bench Verified no longer measures frontier coding capability.

“the first heuristic is that whenever a model apps comes out with like a number, just don't believe them. Just don't.”

03Why Cline built its own evals

Cline found existing evals too rudimentary to reflect real day-to-day programming work, so it built evals from a dataset of real, opted-in user coding sessions (with users paid for their data). The speaker distinguishes this from simple single-turn evals (e.g. a trivia question with a binary answer), which are easy to grade but don't capture the multi-step, open-ended nature of agentic work like reading files, running scripts, and setting up environments.

“we got like we went through a lot of like a massive data sets of like people who had opted in to share their coding usage of client with us and we offered them money”

“a single turn email is very easy to do because it has like a binary answer. It has just like a very limited search space of what the answer could be.”

04Running evals with Terminal Bench and Harbor

Cline adopted Terminal Bench, a Stanford-built suite of 89 approximate real-world programming tasks (race conditions, database issues, infra problems) designed to work with any coding-agent CLI, with some tasks taking 30-40 minutes to run. Harbor, from the Laude Institute, standardizes the infrastructure (isolated VMs with defined CPU/RAM) so all 89 tasks can run in parallel, with the slowest task setting the overall runtime.

“came up with Terminal Bench, which does the same thing, where they came up with like 89 coding problems, which are which are just like very approximate, decent representation of like real world programming problems”

▶ watch at 10:19
05Three zones of improvement when hill climbing

After a benchmark run, failures get triaged into zone one (obvious harness bugs), zone two (nuanced, model-family-specific prompt tuning -- the 'essence' of working with agents), and zone three, a danger zone of straight-up overfitting/cheating to inflate the score. The speaker's closing advice is to hill climb on both the eval score and a genuine vibe check of whether the product actually feels good to use.

“zone three is the danger zone where it's like you're straight up overfitting. So, you're overfitting in the sense of like you're just straight up cheating to get the highest score”

▶ watch at 16:01

“you you got to both pass the vibe check. Like, does it actually feel good to use this product in this model? And at the same time, you also have like a very very very decent uh score”

Commentary (ours)

The three-zone framing (bug fix / nuance / overfit) is a useful lens for any team building internal evals, not just coding agents -- the danger zone criticism implicitly applies to published vendor benchmarks too, which raises the question of how much frontier-lab marketing already lives in zone three (ours).
