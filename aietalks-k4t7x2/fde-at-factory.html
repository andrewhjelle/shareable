If you only read one thing

  • Factory's deployed engineers refuse to do professional-services migration work on customers' behalf and instead act as the feedback loop from customer environments back into the product (c1).
  • Autonomy depends on 'agent readiness' — deterministic validation loops in the code base; roughly 30-40% of gaps are fixable with a single automated pass, the other 60% require workflow changes, and models need this as 'dense reward' to stay on track (c3, c6, c7).
  • Factory reports AI-reviewed code changes are about 87% less likely to hit a bug, and its own code base runs at an internal autonomy ratio in the upper 80% range (c4, c5).

Reyes' case runs from a role boundary (feedback loop, not services) through a technical prerequisite (agent readiness, dense verification) to measured outcomes (bug reduction, autonomy ratio) and a rollout warning; the outcome numbers are self-reported (ours).

Deployed engineers are the product's feedback loop, not a services team

  • Reyes says Factory deliberately avoids taking on professional-services work like codebase modernizations, even when a customer offers to pay and Factory's own tool would be used to do it.
  • Instead, deployed engineers are positioned as 'the tip of the spear' — the channel carrying information from the largest customers' engineering leadership back into product decisions.

“Uh our goal is not to go and actually do that migration on their behalf, even if we happen to be using our product, right?”

02Software factories are built, not bought
Slide at 01:10 · click any slide to enlarge

Software factories are built, not bought

  • Factory frames its offering as building blocks for a customer-owned 'software factory': a pipeline where signals (bug reports, Slack messages, exec directives) flow in, get triaged into plans, converted to code, validated, and shipped, generating new signals in turn.
  • Reyes argues this has to be built by the organization with model-independent tooling, not bought as a locked-in packaged product, partly so the customer retains ownership of the data and traces flowing through it.

“We we like to say this is built, not bought, right?”

said at 06:12
03Agent readiness gates autonomy
Slide at 20:40 · click any slide to enlarge

Agent readiness gates autonomy

  • Reyes defines 'agent readiness' as the density of deterministic validation loops (linters, type checkers, security scans, tests) present in a code base, arguing that agents can operate for longer on harder tasks without human intervention only where verification signals are dense.
  • In practice, he says about 30-40% of readiness gaps can be closed with a single automated fix, while the remaining 60% require actual workflow changes.
  • He ties this directly to how the underlying models were trained: they need dense, verifiable reward signals to stay on track on long-horizon tasks.

“So, if your code base isn't agent ready, you won't see any of the success of the most capable AI systems in the world today, right?”

said at 16:05

“for I'd say maybe 30 to 40% of the low-hanging fruit, you click droid, please fix all of this and it'll go in and it'll fix it, right? But for the other 60% some of them involve workflow changes.”

04Measuring outcomes: bug reduction and autonomy ratio

Measuring outcomes: bug reduction and autonomy ratio

  • Reyes cites a customer-facing ROI figure — code changes that pass through AI code review, QA, and security analysis are roughly 87% less likely to hit a bug — as the kind of business case deployed engineers need to build.
  • He also states Factory's own code base runs at an internal autonomy ratio (ratio of AI to human actions before interruption) in the upper 80% range, though the company estimates only 15-20% overall autonomy today.

“well, we know every code change that flows through that gets AI code review, AI QA, AI security analysis is maybe 87% less likely to hit a bug.”

said at 09:19

“we ourselves have roughly 15 to 20% of what we call like autonomy, and our autonomy ratio is like in the upper 80%, which means the ratio of actions done by humans to AI systems before interruption, right?”

05Rolling out change without building a theme park
Slide at 20:50 · click any slide to enlarge · 1 more from this section below

Rolling out change without building a theme park

  • Using an analogy to Walt Disney's Epcot, Reyes warns that a demonstration environment built too far ahead of an org's actual practices reads as a novelty ('a theme park') that people can't see how to apply to their own work, rather than as a model other teams will copy.

“if you build too much of an advanced example, then people will say, "That's a theme park. That is not at all how the rest of the world works.”

said at 18:07
