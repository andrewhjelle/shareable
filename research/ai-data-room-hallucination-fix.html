The One AI Writing Hack Nobody Talks About.

SpeakerNate B Jones · Nate B Jones Watchyoutu.be/ltbzgzZZmgI · 22 min SourceRecording of this talk at AI Engineer (2026), published on YouTube by the conference. Quotes are verbatim from the talk transcript with the time they were said.
Productivity & learningAgents

If you only read one thing

  • Sullivan & Cromwell had to apologize to a federal bankruptcy judge after filing a motion with dozens of fabricated AI citations, and Nate argues the fix isn't a better prompt — you can't instruct a language model not to hallucinate any more than you can tell autocomplete not to autocomplete.
  • His proposed fix uses the file-manipulation strength of recent agents (Opus 4.7, GPT 5.5): build a bounded local 'project room' and have the agent first produce a source inventory, a conflict log, and a missing-context list before drafting anything.
  • Once that room exists, the actual writing prompt gets short because the agent already knows what's authoritative, what conflicts, and what's missing — Nate calls this treating AI as a colleague instead of a gopher.
01The law firm citation failure

The law firm citation failure

  • Sullivan & Cromwell filed an emergency motion in a bankruptcy case with dozens of fabricated or misquoted AI citations; the firm's own review didn't catch it, opposing counsel did, and the partner who signed the apology letter co-heads the firm's restructuring practice.
  • Nate frames this as an organizational and structural hallucination, not a 2024-style solo-practitioner mistake, and argues the model wasn't the problem, the working environment around it was.

02Why prompting harder doesn't fix it

Why prompting harder doesn't fix it

  • Nate argues there's no separate truth-check pass inside a language model that an instruction like 'don't hallucinate' can hook into, comparing it to telling autocomplete not to autocomplete.
  • Sullivan & Cromwell had access to top-tier AI tooling and still shipped the wrong detail, which he takes as evidence the fix has to be structural rather than a sharper prompt.

03What changed: agents that are good at files

What changed: agents that are good at files

  • Nate says recent agents (Opus 4.7, GPT 5.5) do long-running agentic tasks directly on the file system, walking folder trees, opening files, comparing dates, inspecting metadata, which flips the workflow: the first useful prompt is no longer 'write the document' but 'build me the room to do the work in.'

“agentic tasks, as I've said a lot, but they do it on your file system”

04Build the room before you ask for output

Build the room before you ask for output

  • His recommended first instruction is to have the agent find relevant materials, preserve the originals, and build a data inventory before writing anything.
  • Two more artifacts round this out: a conflict log that surfaces disagreements between sources instead of silently smoothing them over, and a missing-context list that makes gaps visible instead of letting the model invent around them.

05The reframed question for agents

The reframed question for agents

  • Nate's closing argument is that the old question, can the model write the memo or the code, matters less now that models are good enough; the more useful question is whether the agent can help prepare the conditions (sourcing, currency, gaps) under which good work happens.

Commentary (ours)

(ours) The workflow is plausible but self-reported, Nate's evidence for it working is his own experience drafting eight documents at once, not a controlled comparison against prompt-only approaches, so the causal claim that this specifically prevents hallucinations (versus just producing better-organized inputs generally) is unverified.
