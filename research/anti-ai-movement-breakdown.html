The Anti-AI Movement

If you only read one thing

  • YouGov: 58% of Americans don't trust AI versus 35% who do; Pew: only 10% of Americans are more excited than concerned about AI versus 50% more concerned, the worst ratio in that poll.
  • Hundreds of residents at a New Brunswick, NJ planning meeting got a data center project cancelled; the video of the meeting hit 5 million views.
  • Whittemore splits 'anti-AI' into distinct, sometimes contradictory camps (safety/x-risk, capability skeptics, bubblers, artist advocates, child-safety advocates, data-center opponents, job-displacement critics, big-tech haters) and argues the political discourse around AI has not yet hardened.
01The polling behind the backlash

The polling behind the backlash

  • Whittemore cites a YouGov study finding 58% of Americans don't trust AI versus 35% who do, with 45% expecting AI's economic effect to be mostly negative and 63% expecting it to reduce US jobs.
  • He pairs this with a Pew Research poll where the US ranked last among surveyed countries on the ratio of people more excited than concerned about AI.

“A recent Yuggov study found that 58% of Americans said that they don't have trust in AI versus 35% who do.”

02Data centers become a local flashpoint

Data centers become a local flashpoint

  • Whittemore points to a viral example from New Brunswick, New Jersey, where hundreds of residents showed up to a planning meeting and got a proposed data center cancelled, with the video reaching 5 million views.
  • He treats this alongside Nate Silver's commentary as evidence the resistance is growing beyond media narrative.

“hundreds of citizens showed up to a planning meeting about a data center and got the project cancelled. That video shared just a couple of days ago got 5 million views.”

03Nate Silver: white-collar disruption has no precedent

Nate Silver: white-collar disruption has no precedent

  • Whittemore relays Nate Silver's argument that AI's white-collar-first disruption pattern breaks from historical technological transitions, which usually took decades and displaced narrower, less politically powerful groups of workers first.

“Usually these transitions would take decades and not affect white collar workers first who have more political power.”

said at 04:36
04Not one movement: capability skeptics vs. on-the-ground critics

Not one movement: capability skeptics vs. on-the-ground critics

  • Whittemore breaks the anti-AI sentiment into distinct, non-overlapping categories rather than treating it as monolithic.
  • He singles out 'capability skeptics' like Gary Marcus who argue AI is 'just fancy autocomplete,' contrasting them with workplace-specific critics like nurse Hannah Drummond, whose concerns center on a real incident where an AI shift-handoff tool placed a COVID-positive patient next to an immunocompromised one.

05Industry voices push back on industry rhetoric

Industry voices push back on industry rhetoric

  • Whittemore highlights a public response from context engineer Moritz Koinegg to Sam Altman's comment equating the energy cost of training a human to that of training a model, arguing such rhetoric makes AI-industry work 'harder and more dangerous.' Whittemore closes by arguing the political discourse around AI has not yet hardened into fixed camps, leaving room to address specific concerns.

Commentary (ours)

(ours) Whittemore states upfront that he holds different views from most of the anti-AI voices he catalogs and reads the Time cover story as more solvable than alarming; his optimism at the end is his own editorial framing, not a finding from the polls or examples cited earlier in the episode.
