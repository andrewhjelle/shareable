Why half of product managers are in trouble | Nikhyl Singhal (Meta, Google)

If you only read one thing

  • Singhal says the 'information mover' PM whose day was mostly moving status between meetings is becoming obsolete, while builders are having 'the time of their lives' — but he estimates about half of current PMs are non-builders who are 'in trouble.'
  • PM job postings are at a 3-plus-year high right now, but Singhal predicts a 12-24 month cycle where companies shed large staffs (illustratively, 30,000) and rehire far fewer (8,000), all AI-first.
  • The tradeoff he names: relentless pace, an 'equally disappoint everyone' time-allocation problem, and a predicted multi-year setback in industry diversity as people in their 'power years' lack time to spend nights and weekends in coding tools.
01The core split: builders vs. information movers

The core split: builders vs. information movers

  • Singhal argues the product management skill set that mattered three years ago, moving information between a team and its stakeholders, is becoming obsolete, while people who like building things are entering a renaissance with more offers and higher compensation than ever.
  • He estimates roughly half of current PMs fall into the non-builder camp and are, in his words, in trouble.

Job market: hiring boom, layoff wave predicted

03The cost: exhaustion, disappointment math, diversity backslide

The cost: exhaustion, disappointment math, diversity backslide

  • Singhal describes people in their 'power years' as needing to 'equally disappoint everyone', parents, kids, partner, health, work, because there isn't enough time for all of it, and says the pressure to spend nights and weekends in AI tools is going to set back workplace diversity, since women having kids in those same years won't have that time.

04Advice: obsolete yourself, swallow your ego

Advice: obsolete yourself, swallow your ego

  • Singhal's engineering-derived definition of a great practitioner is someone who obsoletes themselves from everything they do, and his career advice extends that to title and seniority: be willing to take a smaller role if that's what it takes to stay current, rather than insisting on matching your last job's level.

“an engineer is someone who obsoletes themselves from everything they do. That's the definition of a great engineer.”
