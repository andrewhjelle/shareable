01Money now comes from tokens, not seats

Money now comes from tokens, not seats

  • Whittemore argues the key economic unit for AI companies shifted this year from the subscription seat to the individual token, so revenue is no longer capped by how many users convert to paid seats but by how many tokens people actually consume via API.
  • He cites his own tool, contextportfolio.ai, racking up about a $5,000 bill in six weeks versus the $200/month he'd paid for a Claude Max seat, as a personal example of the gap.
  • Anthropic's annualized revenue run rate reached $47 billion, up from $3 billion a year earlier, which he attributes to this token-driven growth.

02The subsidy era: why $200/month plans lost money

The subsidy era: why $200/month plans lost money

  • Whittemore describes an 'AI subsidy era' in which flat monthly plans ($100-$300) let power users pull many times more token value than they paid for.
  • He frames the current shift as moving from that subsidy era into a 'token scarcity era.' Uber's CTO said the company burned through its entire 2026 AI budget in just four months, a story Whittemore treats as an early signal the subsidy-era math was already breaking down.

03Providers are switching to per-token billing

Providers are switching to per-token billing

  • GitHub wrote in its Copilot pricing announcement that its current premium-request model is no longer sustainable now that a multi-hour autonomous coding session can cost the same as a quick chat question.
  • Google IO added usage limits and usage-based billing on top of Gemini's paid plans, and Anthropic moved to per-token billing for anyone using Claude outside its own first-party harness.
  • Whittemore ties these announcements directly to what he calls the token shortage era beginning.

04A hard compute constraint, not just a cost spike

A hard compute constraint, not just a cost spike

  • Whittemore asserts the underlying condition is a structural shortage of AI tokens: there simply isn't enough compute to produce all the AI people would want to consume, keeping prices high.
  • Companies that had built internal 'token maxing' leaderboards, including Amazon, are now scrapping them because heavy usage is no longer subsidized.
  • He argues this is a scarcity problem, not a demand problem, and expects it to define the next phase of the industry.

Three responses: cheaper models, more compute, a token tax

  • Market-based responses include Cursor's Composer 2.5, which Whittemore says performs well at a much lower cost than Opus 4.7 and GPT-5.5.

Commentary (ours)

(Ours) The consistent thread across the billing changes, infrastructure deals, and policy chatter is that providers spent the prior year training users to treat tokens as effectively free, and are now unwinding that expectation faster than most enterprise budgeting cycles can adapt, which is plausibly why the Uber and Axios 'sticker shock' stories landed hard.

If usage-based billing becomes the default the way Whittemore describes, the near-term risk for teams that scaled agentic workflows during the subsidy period looks like a budget cliff rather than a gradual ramp, though this is Whittemore's own framing and hasn't been stress-tested against a full quarter of usage-based invoices yet.
