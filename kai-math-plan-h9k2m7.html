How to read it
  • The bands are wide. Every score carries a ±3 RIT error and a percentile range about ten points wide. Fall 2025 math touched 90 at the top of its range. Reading fell 31 percentile points in one spring (Kai said he was tired) and came all the way back. A single-test cutoff on a test with this much noise is a coin flip weighted toward no.
  • Percentile compares Kai to 5th graders. He was rostered in 5th last spring. The question for a 7th-grade math group is whether he can do 7th-grade work. His RIT of 231 is above the national mean for students finishing 7th grade under both the 2020 and 2025 norm sets, and close to end-of-8th under 2025. Verify on the NWEA norm table before quoting exact figures.
  • The school mean is 231, same as Kai. Intercultural is a “district of one school,” so the orange bar is his own class. A 79th national percentile is the middle of this class. The school’s “90th percentile” bar is effectively “top of our class,” which is why three kids cleared it. Expect this; don’t lead with “he’s advanced” in a room where they see the whole distribution.
  • 79th to 90th is roughly six or seven RIT points, about one season of Kai’s normal growth. That is a practice gap, not an ability gap.

How other schools do itThe structure to adopt, not a decision to reverse
  • Illinois law. The Accelerated Placement Act (105 ILCS 5/14A-32, 2018) requires every public district to have a written acceleration policy with published criteria, a parent referral path, multiple measures, and a written transition plan. Oak Park D97 is bound by it. A private school isn’t, but it is the state’s baseline for what fair looks like.
  • CPS policy 302.12 (implements the Act): exit decisions “must be made collaboratively with the school team and the student’s parent or guardian.” cps.edu/sites/cps-policy-rules/policies/300/302/302-12/
  • Trial periods are the norm. Bexley City Schools (OH): “six- to nine-week trial period”; placement “may be reversed without penalty to the student or any negative consequence on the student’s academic record.” Sycamore and Worthington (OH): Written Acceleration Plan with a 6–8 week transition. bexley.us/academics/gifted/acceleration
  • Near-miss cases get more data, not a no. Ames CSD (IA): students “close to meeting criteria” get additional observation-based data before placement. Downers Grove D58 (IL): alternate path of 85th percentile plus a content assessment and teacher rating, instead of one cutoff, and the percentile is measured against the next grade’s norms. amescsd.org · dg58.org
  • The test vendor agrees. NWEA’s guide on using MAP Growth for advanced-placement decisions frames it as one measure among several, with the error band printed on the report. nwea.org · Using MAP Growth for gifted and talented service placement decisions
  • The research. Belin-Blank Center (University of Iowa), A Nation Empowered: acceleration, including single-subject, shows consistently positive academic and social outcomes; the documented risk is under-acceleration, not over. accelerationinstitute.org/nation_empowered
  • Montessori. AMS describes elementary as mastery-based, students “move ahead when ready,” multi-age by design. A trial in the older group is the model working, not an exception to it. amshq.org · Elementary
The proposal, in words Sally can adopt
A six-week trial placement in the Middle School math group, with a short written plan: what Kai needs to keep up with, who checks in, and a review at week six with Beata, Sally, and us. If it isn’t working, he returns to the UE group with no mark against him. This is the same structure Illinois public districts use under the Accelerated Placement Act, and it is how Montessori is meant to work: the child moves when ready, and the adults prepare the environment.
Trial options if they want a smaller step: (a) Kai joins the group for one unit (signed numbers or proportions) and stays if he keeps pace; (b) Kai asks Beata himself for the Middle School work, the child-led path, and the answer becomes a fact for Sally either way; (c) a content check on 6th-grade standards in place of waiting for the fall MAP, the D58 alternate route. Any of the three beats “wait for the next test.”

Meeting lines, in order of use
  • The mechanism, not the philosophy. A child asked for harder work, the work exists in the building, three peers do it in the same time slot, and the only thing in the way is a test he didn’t know mattered.
  • Their sentence, not ours. “You said the criteria are unpublished to spare families anxiety. From where we sit, it did the opposite.”
  • Level, not growth. RIT 231 is above the 7th-grade mean. Don’t lead with the 7-vs-10 growth number; that’s their rebuttal.
  • Andrew’s own trial. Moved up a grade, decided it wasn’t for him, moved back, and it was fine because everyone treated it as something to try.
  • “Other UE goals that warrant refinement.” Ask directly whether there is one. If they name it, that’s the real conversation. If they can’t, the objection is gone.
  • Child-led path, as a question. “Is there a way for Kai himself to ask for the Middle School work and show he’s ready?”
  • Only if it’s going badly: “7th grade is the year that counts for high school.” Nothing more.
