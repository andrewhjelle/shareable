ClassicsPG / PG-13 · kid-viable · streaming unchecked
Built on what already landed
  1. ·
    The Mummy
    1999 · PG-13
    Action-adventure · The closest thing to a fourth Indy movie. Scarab + mummy scares are the Zada flag. Why it matters: a 1932 Universal monster rebuilt as a 90s blockbuster — spot the Indiana Jones borrowings scene by scene.
  2. ·
    October Sky
    1999 · PG
    True story · Kai liked Apollo 13 — this is the kid-version: coal-town boys build rockets. Why it matters: how a memoir becomes a film. Compare to the real Homer Hickam afterward and find what got compressed.
  3. ·
    Hidden Figures
    2016 · PG
    True story · Same NASA thread, and Zada gets the heroes. Not old, but already a classic. Why it matters: who gets left out of the official story and how a film puts them back. Pairs with Apollo 13.
  4. ·
    The General
    1926 · silent
    Keaton comedy · Zada liked Sherlock Jr. — this is Keaton’s best, a train chase the whole way. Why it matters: Keaton did every stunt on a real locomotive, no cuts, no effects. The physical-comedy masterclass.
  5. ·
    The Kid
    1921 · silent
    Chaplin comedy-drama · Chaplin + a kid sidekick. Short (~1 hr). Bridge to City Lights. Why it matters: Chaplin inventing the comedy that makes you cry. Notice the moment the tone turns.
PG-13 canon that works at 11 + 7
  1. ·
    Men in Black
    1997 · PG-13
    Sci-fi comedy · Fast, funny, gross-in-a-fun-way aliens. Both kids. Why it matters: Rick Baker’s creature makeup — practical effects right before everything went digital.
  2. ·
    Mrs. Doubtfire
    1993 · PG-13
    Comedy · Robin Williams at full tilt. A few jokes go over the 7-year-old’s head, harmlessly. Why it matters: a performer carrying a film. The prosthetic makeup won an Oscar; the ad-libs are the real reason it works.
  3. ·
    Pirates of the Caribbean: Black Pearl
    2003 · PG-13
    Swashbuckler · Best of the series. Skeleton-pirate reveal is the Zada flag. Why it matters: a theme-park ride turned into a film by one performance. Early motion-capture on the moonlit skeletons.
  4. ·
    Twister
    1996 · PG-13
    Disaster · Pure spectacle, no real villain. Kai lane, Zada fine. Why it matters: the disaster-film structure laid bare — the chase IS the plot. Mid-90s ILM digital effects.
  5. ·
    Galaxy Quest
    1999 · PG
    Sci-fi comedy · Already in the queue — counts as a classic now. Why it matters: parody that loves its target. The lesson is affectionate genre deconstruction.
Older PG classics worth trying
  1. ·
    Who Framed Roger Rabbit
    1988 · PG
    Noir-cartoon hybrid · Zada gets the toons, Kai gets the mystery. Judge Doom’s ending is intense. Why it matters: live action + hand-drawn animation composited frame by frame, pre-digital. Film noir plot for kids.
  2. ·
    Groundhog Day
    1993 · PG
    Comedy · The loop premise lands at 11. Rewatchable by design. Why it matters: structure lesson — the same day told differently each time. Every time-loop story since is quoting it.
  3. ·
    The Iron Giant
    1999 · PG
    Animated · Wild Robot-tender before The Wild Robot. Short. Why it matters: Brad Bird before Pixar; the last great hand-drawn studio film before the 3D takeover.
  4. ·
    Honey, I Shrunk the Kids
    1989 · PG
    Family adventure · Backyard as jungle. Both kids. Why it matters: forced perspective and oversized sets — effects you can explain and then try with a camera.
  5. ·
    Cool Runnings
    1993 · PG
    Sports comedy · Feel-good true-ish story. Zero flags. Why it matters: “based on a true story” and what that license covers. Fact-check it together after.
  6. ·
    Star Wars: A New Hope
    1977 · PG
    Space adventure · Only if they haven’t seen it — Zero Company is on Andrew’s games list, so the universe is live. Why it matters: where the modern blockbuster begins. Wipes, the opening crawl, and a plot lifted from Kurosawa’s Hidden Fortress.
  7. ·
    North by Northwest
    1959 · PG
    Hitchcock thriller · The fun Hitchcock: crop duster, Mount Rushmore. Kai yes; Zada maybe. Why it matters: Hitchcock’s suspense rule — the audience knows more than the hero. Watch for it in the crop-duster scene.
